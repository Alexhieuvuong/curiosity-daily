## Connect the dots

A central bank with a **dual mandate** faces a permanent temptation: the policy that looks optimal today becomes a **time inconsistency** problem tomorrow, which is exactly why politicians try to **insulate** it from short-term pressure. When a bank is solvent but suffers **illiquidity**, the **lender of last resort** can lend against good collateral — but if the problem is true **insolvency**, no loan will fix it, and **supervisory** forbearance only delays the reckoning. A **hawkish** governor fights inflation by raising rates, yet even that move depends on **anchoring** expectations, since credibility is a **self-fulfilling** asset: people behave well only if they believe the bank will act. Meanwhile, on the fiscal side, an ageing **cohort** turns a **pay-as-you-go** promise into an **unfunded liability**, and each **intergenerational transfer** raises questions about long-run **sustainability**. None of this is **endogenous** to a single model; it demands a **holistic**, **intertemporal** view in which **empirical** evidence and **normative** judgment are weighed together rather than treated as **incommensurate** rivals.
