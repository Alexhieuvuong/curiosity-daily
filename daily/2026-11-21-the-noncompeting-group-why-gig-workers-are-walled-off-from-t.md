# The Noncompeting Group: Why Gig Workers Are Walled Off From the Rest of the Labor Market

> A 19th-century Irish economist named the trap long before the first app existed — and the gig economy may have built a new wall around it.

## Why this is interesting
We usually argue about gig work as a question of *labels* — contractor or employee. But underneath the label sits a structural fact: some groups of workers cannot easily cross into other groups, so wages stop competing with each other. Gig work is interesting because it appears to manufacture that wall, then presents it as freedom.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: a labor market only disciplines wages when workers can move between jobs and employers can bid for them. Labor market segmentation is the division of the market by occupation, geography, or industry — and one sharp form of it defines groups "with little or no crossover capability," such that members of one segment cannot easily join another [3]. When that crossover fails, the same work can command different wages in different segments [3]. The 19th-century political economist John Elliott Cairnes called these "noncompeting groups" [3]. A related idea, the dual labour market, splits the aggregate market into a primary sector and a secondary sector [3]. One reading is that gig work sits in that secondary tier — and the classification fight is really a fight about whether the wall stays up.

## Break it into pieces
- **Is the wall legal or economic?** The contractor/employee classification debate is explicitly about legal status [2] — but the *effect* may be segmentation. Which one is doing the real work?
- **Who can cross?** Cairnes's "noncompeting groups" hinge on crossover capability [3]. What specifically blocks a gig driver from moving into a primary-sector job — credentials, scheduling, benefits portability, or something else?
- **What does the third box do?** California's Proposition 22 created a third classification — contractors with certain benefits like minimum wage and mileage reimbursement [2]. Does a third box *soften* the wall or *formalize* it?
- **Who benefits from the wall?** Segmentation changes who bears risk. Trace it.

## Follow the incentives
Companies classify workers as "independent contractors" [2] — and the incentive is plain: employee status would legally require the full suite of benefits, including time-and-a-half overtime, paid sick time, employer-provided health care, bargaining rights, and unemployment insurance [2]. Organized labor advocates lobby for employee classification precisely because those obligations follow it [2]. So the two sides are not arguing about words; they are arguing about who absorbs cost and risk. The worker sits in the middle: contractor status may offer flexibility, but it also means the benefits listed above are not legally owed [2]. Note the asymmetry the sources describe — companies classify their workers as independent contractors while advocates push for employee status [2].

## How it echoes elsewhere
Illustrative: the same "noncompeting group" mechanic appears in **credentialed professions**, where entry barriers stop outsiders from bidding down wages — the wall is a license rather than a contract. It also echoes **internal vs. external labor markets** inside large firms, where insiders on a primary track and outsiders on a secondary track rarely trade places. In both, one could argue the wage gap is not a market failure but the market working exactly as the wall allows.

## A real-world case
California's Proposition 22, approved by popular vote in 2020, created a third worker classification for gig-worker-drivers: contractors, but retaining certain benefits such as minimum wage and mileage reimbursement [2]. Read through the segmentation lens, this is a fascinating hybrid — it keeps the contractor wall (no full employee suite) while bolting on a few primary-sector protections. It is, in effect, an attempt to let a noncompeting group keep its separate status while softening the worst of the secondary tier.

## Second-order effects
If a third box works, one might expect it to spread — other platforms and sectors could lobby for their own bespoke classification, fragmenting the labor market into many small noncompeting segments rather than two clean tiers. That fragmentation could weaken the bargaining power that comes from a large, unified group, since each segment negotiates alone. It may also make crossover *harder*, not easier: a worker with a gig-specific classification may find their experience doesn't transfer cleanly to primary-sector roles. My reasoning, following from [2] and [3]: the more boxes, the more walls.

## A question to sit with
If a third classification gives gig workers *some* benefits but keeps them a noncompeting group, is that progress — or a more durable way of keeping the wall standing?

## Go deeper
- Compare Cairnes's "noncompeting groups" [3] with modern credentialing: which wall is harder to climb, a contract or a license?
- Ask what "crossover capability" would actually require for a gig worker — portable benefits, transferable records, or something else entirely.
- Debate whether a third box like Proposition 22 [2] is a template for the future or a one-off political compromise.
