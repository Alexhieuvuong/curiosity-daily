# Why Your Attention Is Priced Like a Commodity, Not a Choice

> The moment you open an app, you're not a consumer — you're a resource being extracted.

## Why this is interesting
We tend to think of attention as a personal, almost spiritual resource — something we "pay" to things we care about. But the economics of attention treats it far more coldly: as a scarce input that advertising-driven companies compete to capture and sell. Understanding this shift reframes your daily scrolling as a market transaction, not a habit.

## First principles
The attention economy rests on a simple premise: human attention is scarce, and information is abundant [1]. In classical economics, scarcity creates value — and here, the scarce good is not the content you consume but the time and mental focus you give it. Advertising-driven companies are incentivized to maximize the time and attention users give to their product, because that attention is what they sell to advertisers [1]. So the product you see — the feed, the video, the headline — is not the good. Your attention is the good, and the content is merely the bait.

## Break it into pieces
- **Who is the real customer?** The user provides attention; the advertiser pays for it. The platform is the intermediary that prices and sells that attention.
- **What is "attention" actually worth?** It's not a fixed number — it depends on how targetable, engaged, and verifiable the audience is. Digital technology lowered the cost of tracking users, making attention more precisely measurable and therefore more valuable [5].
- **Is all attention equal?** No. A distracted glance is worth less than a focused watch. Platforms design interfaces to convert passive exposure into active engagement.
- **Why is the content free?** Because the price of the content is zero, but the price of your attention is hidden — you pay by giving up time and data, not money.
- **What breaks the model?** If attention becomes too fragmented, or if users can verify and avoid ads, the value of the attention drops — which is why platforms constantly fight ad-blockers and push toward "native" content.

## Follow the incentives
The platform wants you to stay longer, because more time means more ad impressions and more data to sell [1]. The advertiser wants to reach the right person at the right moment, and digital technology drastically reduced the cost of tracking and verifying who you are and what you do [5]. The user, meanwhile, gets free content but bears the cost of lost time and privacy. The risk is asymmetrical: platforms profit from your engagement, advertisers profit from your behavior, and you carry the opportunity cost of what you could have done instead. This is why platforms invest so heavily in recommendation algorithms — they are not serving you; they are optimizing the extraction of your attention.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same mechanic appears in **environmental economics**: The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity report compiled monetary values for ecosystem services, and those specific dollar figures received the most media attention — not the methodological nuance [4]. In both cases, a complex, qualitative resource (attention, biodiversity) gets reduced to a price tag, and that price tag then drives decisions. Another echo is in **media studies**, which draws on political economy to ask who owns media and whose interests are served — a lens that treats attention not as a personal experience but as a structural resource shaped by ownership and power [2].

## A real-world case
Kaitlin Bennett, a gun rights activist, received media attention in 2018 for open-carrying an AR-10 rifle at Kent State University after graduating [3]. She is the face of the YouTube channel Liberty Hangout, which was founded by her husband and promotes the Austrian school of economics [3]. The case shows how individuals, not just corporations, can become prominent figures in the attention economy: a provocative act can capture attention in a crowded media landscape, which can then be converted into platform influence and ideological reach.

## Second-order effects
If attention is a scarce commodity, then the market for it creates perverse incentives: outrage, novelty, and conflict are cheap to produce and highly effective at capturing attention. That means the content that wins is not necessarily the most truthful or useful — it is the most attention-efficient. Over time, this can shift what kinds of media get produced, what kinds of voices get amplified, and what kinds of facts get funded. It also means that the "free" content you consume is subsidized by your own cognitive labor — you are working, in a sense, for the platform every time you scroll. The deeper consequence is that attention scarcity may not be solved by more information, but by better filters — and whoever controls the filters controls the market.

## A question to sit with
If your attention is a commodity that platforms extract and sell, what would it mean to actually own it — and who would enforce that ownership?

2. **Extraction** — (noun, /ɪkˈstrækʃən/) — the act of taking something out, often with effort or cost. _Example: Platforms optimize for the extraction of user attention through endless feeds._
3. **Incentive** — (noun, /ɪnˈsɛntɪv/) — something that motivates or encourages action. _Example: Advertising revenue creates a strong incentive for platforms to maximize user time._
4. **Asymmetrical** — (adjective, /ˌeɪsɪˈmɛtrɪkəl/) — not balanced or equal on both sides. _Example: The risk is asymmetrical: platforms profit, users lose time._
5. **Intermediary** — (noun, /ˌɪntəˈmiːdiəri/) — a middleman or go-between. _Example: The platform acts as an intermediary between advertisers and users._
6. **Verifiable** — (adjective, /ˈvɛrɪfaɪəbl/) — able to be confirmed or proven true. _Example: Digital tracking made user attention more verifiable, increasing its value to advertisers._
7. **Perverse** — (adjective, /pəˈvɜːs/) — contrary to what is expected or reasonable; counterproductive. _Example: The attention market creates perverse incentives for outrage and novelty._
8. **Opportunity cost** — (noun, /ˌɒpəˈtjuːnɪti kɒst/) — the value of the best alternative foregone when making a choice. _Example: Every minute spent scrolling carries an opportunity cost of what you could have done instead._
9. **Fragmented** — (adjective, /ˈfræɡmɛntɪd/) — broken into pieces; scattered. _Example: Fragmented attention across many platforms is harder to monetize than focused attention._
10. **Subsidized** — (verb, /ˈsʌbsɪdaɪzd/) — supported financially, often indirectly. _Example: Free content is subsidized by the user's own attention and data._
11. **Cognitive** — (adjective, /ˈkɒɡnɪtɪv/) — related to mental processes like thinking, knowing, and judging. _Example: Scrolling is a form of cognitive labor that platforms profit from._
12. **Notoriety** — (noun, /ˌnəʊtəˈraɪəti/) — the state of being famous for something bad or controversial. _Example: Bennett's notoriety was a deliberate investment in attention._
