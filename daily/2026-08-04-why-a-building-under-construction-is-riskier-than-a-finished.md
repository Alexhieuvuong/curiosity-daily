# Why a Building Under Construction Is Riskier Than a Finished One

> Insurance doesn't just price what could go wrong — it prices *when* the thing is most fragile.

## Why this is interesting
A half-built skyscraper is a strange object: it's not yet a building, but it's already a pile of expensive materials, exposed machinery, and unfinished systems. Builder's risk insurance exists precisely because this in-between state has its own logic of danger — different from either an empty lot or a finished structure.

## First principles
Insurance is a mechanism for transferring the financial consequence of an uncertain loss. Builder's risk insurance (also called Contractor's All Risk, or CAR) is a type of property insurance that indemnifies against damage to buildings *while they are under construction* [1]. It protects a person's or organization's insurable interest in materials, fixtures, and equipment being used in construction or renovation, should those items suffer physical loss or damage from a covered cause [1]. The core mechanic is temporal: the policy covers a *phase* — the construction period — not a permanent state.

## Break it into pieces
- **What is "insurable interest"?** You can only insure something you stand to lose. A contractor, owner, or lender each has a different stake in the unfinished structure, so the policy must define who is covered and for what [1].
- **What counts as a "covered cause"?** The policy covers physical loss or damage from a covered cause, but the exact list depends on the policy [1]. The unfinished state makes some causes more likely and others more severe.
- **Who is the insured?** The policy protects a person's or organization's insurable interest in materials, fixtures, and equipment being used in construction or renovation [1]. The owner, the general contractor, subcontractors, and sometimes the lender may all be named.
- **What is the "risk" being priced?** Not just the probability of a loss, but the *vulnerability* of the asset during a specific window of construction. A finished building has fire doors and sprinklers; a construction site may have neither. (Illustrative: this contrast is general knowledge, not sourced.)
- **How does this differ from standard property insurance?** Standard property insurance assumes a stable, completed structure. Builder's risk assumes a changing, incomplete one — which is precisely why it is a distinct product [1].

## Follow the incentives
The owner pays the premium because a fire or collapse during construction could wipe out their investment before it ever generates income. The contractor is incentivized to buy coverage because their liability for damage to the project could be enormous — and because lenders often require insurance as a condition of financing. The insurer, meanwhile, prices the policy based on the risks of the construction phase. The party bearing the most risk is the insurer, but only for a defined period — after completion, the risk shifts back to a standard property policy. This is a classic example of risk being *temporally segmented*: the same building has different risk profiles at different stages of its life.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same logic appears in **credit risk**: a lender's exposure is tied to the borrower's ability to repay, and higher credit risk is associated with higher borrowing costs [3]. Likewise, in **cyber insurance**, the product protects businesses from risks relating to information technology infrastructure and activities [2]. In all three, the insurer or lender is not pricing a thing but a *state of fragility*.

## A real-world case
The sources do not provide a specific named construction disaster, so here is an **Illustrative:** scenario. A developer is building a 20-story office tower. During the framing stage, a fire breaks out in a storage area containing insulation and wooden formwork. Because the building has no permanent fire suppression system yet, the fire spreads through open elevator shafts and damages several floors of steel and concrete. The builder's risk policy covers the cost of repairing the damage and replacing the materials — but only if the policy explicitly lists fire as a covered cause and only up to the policy limit. The insurer, having priced the policy knowing that construction sites lack active fire protection, charged a premium reflecting that elevated risk. The developer, meanwhile, faces not just repair costs but *delay*.

## Second-order effects
Because builder's risk insurance is priced per phase, it creates incentives for *construction sequencing*: contractors may choose to install fire-resistant materials earlier, or schedule hot work (welding) during lower-risk periods, to reduce premiums. Insurers, in turn, may require safety audits or inspections as a condition of coverage, effectively outsourcing some risk management back to the insured. This is a subtle but powerful feedback loop: the price of risk changes behavior, which changes the risk itself. A second-order effect is on *lending*: since lenders often require builder's risk coverage, the availability and cost of insurance can influence whether a project gets financed at all — meaning the insurance market indirectly shapes which buildings get built, and where.

## A question to sit with
If insurance prices *vulnerability over time*, not just *probability of loss*, then who should bear the cost of a risk that is created by the construction process itself — the owner, the contractor, the insurer, or society?

6. **Moral hazard** — (noun phrase, /ˈmɔːrəl ˈhæzərd/) — the risk that a party will take more risks because they are protected against the consequences. _Example: If the insurer covers all fire damage, the contractor may be less careful with welding — a classic moral hazard._

11. **Condition of coverage** — (noun phrase, /kənˈdɪʃən əv ˈkʌvərɪdʒ/) — a requirement that must be met for an insurance policy to apply. _Example: The insurer made a weekly safety inspection a condition of coverage._
