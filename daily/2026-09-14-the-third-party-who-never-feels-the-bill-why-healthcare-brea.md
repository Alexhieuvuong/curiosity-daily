# The Third Party Who Never Feels the Bill: Why Healthcare Breaks the Rules of Economics

> In most markets, the person who chooses the good also pays for it. In healthcare, those two people are almost never the same — and that single split rewires everything.

## Why this is interesting
Economics has a reliable default: prices coordinate behavior because the buyer feels the cost. Healthcare economics is the discipline built around the discovery that this default fails [1]. When an insurer — not the patient — is financially responsible for the care consumed, price and quality get "hidden" from the person making the decision [1]. That one structural quirk is why healthcare is studied as its own field rather than as a normal market.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic of any market: a transaction requires someone who *chooses* and someone who *pays*, and when they're the same person, price does its job. Health economics begins by noting that in healthcare these roles split apart. The "third-party agent" is the patient's health insurer, who is financially responsible for the goods and services the insured patient consumes [1]. Kenneth Arrow's seminal 1963 article is often credited with founding the discipline precisely by drawing conceptual distinctions between health and other goods [1]. The distinguishing features he identified include extensive government intervention, intractable uncertainty, asymmetric information, barriers to entry, externalities, and the presence of that third-party agent [1]. Once the payer and the chooser are separated, the ordinary signal — price — stops reaching the decision-maker.

## Break it into pieces
- **Who chooses vs. who pays:** If the patient consumes and the insurer pays, what happens to the price signal that normally disciplines demand?
- **Hidden quality:** The sources note price *and quality* are "often hidden" by the third-party payer system [1] — so how does a patient judge value without either signal?
- **Measuring the unmeasurable:** QALYs (Quality Adjusted Life Years) are a common yardstick for treatments but are "very difficult to measure" and rest on assumptions "often unreasonable" [1]. How do you price a benefit you can't cleanly quantify?
- **Uncertainty and information gaps:** Asymmetric information and intractable uncertainty are baked into the field [1] — who knows what, and when?
- **Spillovers:** Externalities arise frequently in health, e.g., infectious disease or opioid abuse [1] — costs and benefits that land on people who never transacted.

## Follow the incentives
Trace the money and the motives. The **patient** consumes care but, shielded by insurance, doesn't bear the full marginal cost — so the usual restraint of price weakens [1]. The **insurer** is the third-party agent financially responsible for what the insured consumes [1], giving it a strong incentive to manage, gate, and price risk it cannot directly observe. The **provider** operates in a setting marked by barriers to entry and asymmetric information [1] — the clinician typically knows more about the treatment than the patient does. And the **public** bears costs it never chose, because externalities like infectious disease or opioid abuse spill onto people beyond the decision-maker [1]. Note the deeper irony: because price and quality are hidden, the very signals that would let each party act in its own interest are muffled [1].

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same "decoupling of chooser and payer" pattern shows up wherever a third party settles the bill — any such arrangement distorts the price signal the way healthcare's third-party payer system does [1]. It also echoes in **public goods and externality problems**: when one person's choice imposes costs or benefits on others, as with infectious disease or opioid abuse, the market price no longer captures the full story [1]. In both cases the fix isn't a better price tag — it's recognizing that the transaction has more parties than the receipt shows.

## A real-world case
The source material's clearest concrete illustration is the externality itself: making an effort to avoid catching the common cold affects people other than the decision maker, and finding sustainable, humane, and effective solutions to the opioid epidemic is a problem whose costs fall on far more people than those directly involved [1]. Neither the cold-avoider nor the opioid user is transacting only with themselves — the consequences radiate outward to strangers who never agreed to anything [1]. That is the third-party problem in its purest form: a decision whose bill lands on someone else's table.

## Second-order effects
If price and quality are hidden by the third-party payer system [1], then competition on price and quality is structurally weakened — providers can't easily win on signals buyers can't see, and patients can't easily reward value they can't perceive. This pushes activity toward other battlegrounds: networks, gatekeeping, and the management of risk rather than the transparent pricing of care. It also explains why the field leans so heavily on proxy measures like QALYs [1] — when the real signal is obscured, people reach for a constructed one, even knowing its assumptions are "often unreasonable" [1]. And because externalities are frequent [1], private decisions systematically under- or over-count costs that the public ultimately absorbs.

## A question to sit with
If the person who chooses care is never the person who pays for it, can any price — however cleverly designed — ever do the coordinating work that prices do in ordinary markets?
