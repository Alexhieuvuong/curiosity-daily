# The Noncompeting Groups: Why Gig Work Grows When the Economy Shrinks

> A downturn doesn't just push people toward gig platforms — it reveals that the labor market was never one market at all.

## Why this is interesting
We tend to imagine "the labor market" as a single pool where workers flow toward the best-paying jobs. But economists have long argued it is actually a patchwork of segments with "little or no crossover capability" [2]. Gig platforms sit right at the seams of that patchwork — and research suggests they swell precisely when the offline economy sours [4]. Understanding why turns a familiar headline ("gig work is booming") into a structural story about who can move between segments and who cannot.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: a labor market is a matching machine between people who can do work and organizations that need it done. Segmentation theory says that machine is not one machine but several, divided by occupation, geography, or industry [2]. When the segments are sealed — when members of one "cannot easily join another" — you get what 19th-century Irish political economist John Elliott Cairnes called "noncompeting groups," and different segments can receive different wages for the same work [2]. A related model, the dual labour market, splits the aggregate market into a primary sector and a secondary sector [2]. Gig and temporary work sit squarely in that secondary zone: arrangements limited to a certain period, driven by the employer's needs, often without the benefits permanent employees receive [3].

## Break it into pieces
- What actually seals a segment — skills, geography, credentials, or something less visible like who will hire whom?
- Why would a *downturn* push workers toward platforms rather than away from them?
- If gig platforms lower barriers to entry, do they dissolve segmentation or simply redraw it?
- Who captures the value when a worker moves from a permanent job into a contingent one?
- Does "flexibility" describe the worker's freedom or the employer's cost structure?

## Follow the incentives
Employers bear less risk under contingent arrangements: temporary workers "usually" get benefits only if they are permanent, a deliberate cost-cutting choice [3]. Platforms profit by matching at scale while keeping workers classified as independent, so the fixed costs of employment sit with the worker. Workers, facing a shock to offline income, rationally seek the lowest-barrier option — and gig platforms advertise exactly that, "low barriers to entry" enabling flexible, contingent work whenever and, online, wherever the worker desires [4]. The academic evidence points to economic downturns and "associated financial stressors in the offline economy, for example, unemployment" as a driver of platform participation, with local economic conditions significantly affecting labor supply on both the intensive and extensive margins, and with heterogeneous effects across county characteristics [4]. Meanwhile, policymakers have begun responding — the paper notes proposals to protect gig workers, such as California Assembly Bill 5 [4].

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same "noncompeting groups" logic appears in migration. Gig platforms concentrate around large cities and "seem to depend as much on the cheap labor of migrants and minorities as on investment capital and permissive governments" [5], and researchers describe a "platformization of low-wage labor markets that are an integral component of migration infrastructures" [5]. In both cases, a barrier — a border, a credential, a benefit threshold — keeps two pools of workers from competing, and an intermediary profits from the gap.

## A real-world case
The 2020 study combined data from a leading online labor market with archival sources such as the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and found that local economic conditions significantly shape both how many people join online labor markets and how intensively they work, with effects varying across county characteristics [4]. In other words, a weak local job market didn't just idle workers — it redirected their labor onto a platform. (The sources do not name the specific platform, so treat the mechanism, not the brand, as the finding.)

## Second-order effects
If downturns reliably push labor onto platforms, then gig participation becomes a *countercyclical buffer* — a shock absorber that keeps people earning but also normalizes contingent status. Over time, that could deepen segmentation rather than dissolve it: the very "low barriers to entry" that make platforms attractive in a slump may make it harder for workers to climb back into the primary sector when the economy recovers. And because platforms depend on migrant and minority labor [5], the buffer is unevenly distributed — borne most by those with the fewest alternatives.

## A question to sit with
If gig platforms lower the barriers *into* work, do they also quietly raise the barriers *out* of it — and who would ever measure that?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **segmentation** — (noun, /ˌsɛɡmənˈteɪʃən/) — the division of something into distinct parts. _Example: Labor market segmentation splits workers into groups that rarely compete for the same jobs._
2. **noncompeting groups** — (noun phrase, /nɒn kəmˈpiːtɪŋ ɡruːps/) — sets of workers who cannot easily move into one another's jobs. _Example: Cairnes used "noncompeting groups" to explain why identical work could pay differently._
3. **dual labour market** — (noun phrase, /ˈdjuːəl ˈleɪbə ˈmɑːkɪt/) — a model splitting the market into a primary and a secondary sector. _Example: The dual labour market frames gig work as a secondary-sector arrangement._
4. **contingent employment** — (noun phrase, /kənˈtɪndʒənt ɪmˈplɔɪmənt/) — work that depends on demand and is not permanent. _Example: Platforms let workers engage in contingent employment whenever they choose._
5. **intensive margin** — (noun phrase, /ɪnˈtɛnsɪv ˈmɑːdʒɪn/) — how much existing participants work. _Example: A downturn may raise the intensive margin as current gig workers take on more hours._
6. **extensive margin** — (noun phrase, /ɪkˈstɛnsɪv ˈmɑːdʒɪn/) — whether new people enter a market at all. _Example: Rising unemployment can push the extensive margin up as newcomers join platforms._
7. **heterogeneous** — (adjective, /ˌhɛtərəˈdʒiːniəs/) — varied; not uniform. _Example: The effects of local conditions were heterogeneous across county characteristics._
8. **countercyclical** — (adjective, /ˌkaʊntəˈsaɪklɪkəl/) — moving opposite to the economic cycle. _Example: Gig participation can look countercyclical, rising as the offline economy weakens._
9. **platformization** — (noun, /ˌplætfɔːməɪˈzeɪʃən/) — the process by which an activity becomes mediated by digital platforms. _Example: Researchers describe the platformization of low-wage labor markets._
10. **infrastructure** — (noun, /ˈɪnfrəstrʌktʃə/) — the underlying systems that enable activity. _Example: Migration infrastructures include the platforms that route workers to jobs._
11. **conduit** — (noun, /ˈkɒndjuɪt/) — a channel through which something passes. _Example: Gig platforms act as a conduit between migrant labor and urban demand._
12. **permissive** — (adjective, /pəˈmɪsɪv/) — allowing freedom of action, often by not restricting. _Example: Platforms rely on permissive governments that tolerate their labor arrangements._
