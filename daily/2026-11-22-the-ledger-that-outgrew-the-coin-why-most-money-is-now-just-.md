# The Ledger That Outgrew the Coin: Why Most Money Is Now Just a Balance Somewhere

> The coins in your pocket are the smallest part of the story — most of the money you "have" is a number on someone else's books.

## Why this is interesting
We intuitively picture money as *stuff* — notes, coins, metal. But the definition of money is far looser: it is "any item or verifiable record that is generally accepted as payment" [2]. That phrase "verifiable record" quietly opens the door to money that is nothing but an entry in an account. In developed countries, that kind of money already dwarfs the physical kind [2].

## First principles
Start from the function, not the object. Money is a means of fulfilling exchange *indirectly and in general* rather than directly, as with barter [1]. To do that, an item or record must be generally accepted as payment for goods, services, and debts such as taxes [2]. The distinguishing functions are: a medium of exchange, a unit of account, a store of value, and sometimes a standard of deferred payment [2]. Crucially, money can be physical (coins and notes) *or* exist as a written or electronic account [1]. Its value can come from intrinsic worth (commodity money), a legal promise (representative money), or mere nominal value (fiat money) [1]. So the irreducible mechanic is: **a shared, trusted record of who owes what to whom** — the physical token is optional.

## Break it into pieces
- What actually makes a "verifiable record" trustworthy enough to circulate as money?
- Why does the source distinguish *commodity*, *representative*, and *fiat* money — and what changes when you cross from one to the next [1]?
- If bank money "forms by far the largest part of broad money in developed countries" [2], what is the physical currency actually *for*?
- Who guarantees a balance held in a bank account, versus a note in your hand?

## Follow the incentives
The source notes that nearly all contemporary money systems are based on unbacked fiat money without use value, whose value derives from social convention and legal tender status — it "must be accepted as a form of payment within the boundaries of the country, for 'all debts, public and private'" [2]. That is a powerful incentive structure: a government declares what counts, and the declaration itself does much of the work. Meanwhile, bank money — balances on the books of financial institutions, convertible into notes or used for cashless payment — constitutes the bulk of broad money [2]. My reading: the institutions holding those balances sit at the center of the system, because they maintain the records everyone else treats as money.

## How it echoes elsewhere
*Illustrative:* the same "record, not object" logic can be seen by analogy in **accounting**, where a balance is a claim recorded on a ledger rather than a thing you can hold — and in **property law**, where ownership is commonly a recognized entry in a registry, not physical possession. In both, the *record's* credibility, not the object's, is what people rely on. (These are analogies offered for comparison, not claims drawn from the sources.)

## A real-world case
The sources give a structural example rather than a single episode: in developed countries, broad money is dominated by bank money — checking and savings balances held on institutional books — with currency in circulation forming only a part [2]. *Illustrative:* if you paid a friend for lunch by transferring from your checking account, no note or coin moved; what changed was two numbers on a bank's ledger. That everyday act is the system working exactly as the source describes.

## Second-order effects
If money is fundamentally a trusted record, then the *stability of the record-keeper* becomes a monetary question, not just a banking one. The academic source sharpens this: it argues "money" is best conceived as a constitutional phenomenon, so that changes in the nature of money represent changes in political and constitutional arrangements [5]. Following that reasoning, a shift in *how* money is recorded — from coin to account to digital token — is never merely technical; it redistributes who controls the ledger and therefore who holds power.

## A question to sit with
If most money is already just a balance on someone's books, what is genuinely *new* about a digital currency — or is it only a change in who keeps the ledger?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **fiat** — (noun/adjective, /ˈfiː.æt/) — money whose value rests on official declaration rather than backing by a commodity. _Example: Fiat money has no use value; its worth comes from legal tender status._
2. **intrinsic** — (adjective, /ɪnˈtrɪn.zɪk/) — belonging to a thing by its own nature, not from outside. _Example: Commodity money is valued for its intrinsic worth._
3. **nominal** — (adjective, /ˈnɒm.ɪ.nəl/) — existing in name or stated value only, not in real substance. _Example: Fiat money carries only nominal value._
4. **verifiable** — (adjective, /ˈver.ɪ.faɪ.ə.bəl/) — able to be checked or proven true. _Example: Money can be any verifiable record accepted as payment._
5. **medium of exchange** — (noun phrase, /ˈmiː.di.əm əv ɪksˈtʃeɪndʒ/) — the thing used to intermediate trades instead of barter. _Example: A medium of exchange lets you sell without needing a direct swap._
6. **unit of account** — (noun phrase, /ˈjuː.nɪt əv əˈkaʊnt/) — the standard measure in which prices are stated. _Example: A shared unit of account makes prices comparable._
7. **store of value** — (noun phrase, /stɔːr əv ˈvæl.juː/) — something that holds purchasing power over time. _Example: Inflation erodes money's role as a store of value._
8. **legal tender** — (noun phrase, /ˈliː.ɡəl ˈten.dər/) — money that must legally be accepted for debts. _Example: Legal tender status obliges acceptance for all debts, public and private._
9. **broad money** — (noun phrase, /brɔːd ˈmʌn.i/) — the widest measure of money supply, including bank balances. _Example: Bank money forms most of broad money in developed economies._
10. **convertible** — (adjective, /kənˈvɜː.tə.bəl/) — able to be exchanged for something else. _Example: Bank balances are convertible into physical notes._
11. **convention** — (noun, /kənˈven.ʃən/) — a shared, accepted practice or agreement. _Example: Fiat money's value derives from social convention._
12. **monetary** — (adjective, /ˈmʌn.ɪ.tər.i/) — relating to money or the money supply. _Example: Monetary systems shape how value is recorded and trusted._
