# The Economy That Isn't Counted — and Why Governments Keep Recalculating It

> If half your country's work happens off the books, does it count as an economy — or only when the statisticians decide it does?

## Why this is interesting
The informal economy is defined by what it *escapes*: taxation and government monitoring [1]. Yet it is not small or marginal — it provides critical economic opportunities for the poor and has been expanding rapidly since the 1960s [1]. The strange part is the accounting: the same activity can be invisible in one country's GDP and explicitly counted in another's, which means "the size of the economy" is partly a measurement choice, not just a fact.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: an economic transaction is *informal* when it is neither taxed nor monitored by any form of government [1]. That single condition — escaping the state's ledger — is what defines the category, not the type of work. A consequence follows immediately: because informal activity isn't monitored, it typically isn't included in a country's gross national product (GNP) or gross domestic product (GDP) [1]. So the boundary between "formal" and "informal" is drawn by the state's capacity and willingness to observe, not by whether value was actually created. Change what the state counts, and the measured economy changes — even if nothing real changed.

## Break it into pieces
- **Definition problem:** the informal sector is sometimes stigmatized as troublesome and unmanageable, yet it also supplies critical opportunities for the poor [1] — is it a problem or a lifeline?
- **Measurement problem:** if informal activity is excluded from GDP [1], what exactly are we comparing when we rank economies?
- **The counting choice:** Italy has included estimates of informal activity in its GDP since 1987, swelling GDP by an estimated 18% [1] — so how much of a "national economy" is an estimate?
- **The convergence problem:** academic work argues that current definitions of the informal economy fail to converge around a unitary construct [5] — if researchers can't agree on what it is, how do policymakers target it?
- **The linkage problem:** the informal economy is not separate from the formal one; the policy question is how to promote more equitable linkages between them [4].

## Follow the incentives
Who benefits from informality, and who bears the risk? Workers in the informal sector gain income but face serious concerns about the lack of protections afforded to them [2]. Local governments in some regions of China have adopted policies designed to encourage the informal economy to alleviate unemployment [2] — the state itself may tolerate or promote what it doesn't tax, because the alternative is joblessness. Meanwhile, formal-sector actors benefit from the flexibility: China's rise as a global center of manufacturing owes, in part, to its willingness to trade in the informal economy, also known as system D trade [2]. And the statisticians have their own incentive: including informal activity raises measured GDP, as Italy's 18% bump illustrates [1]. Each party — worker, local official, manufacturer, statistical agency — is responding to a different payoff.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same mechanic — value that exists but isn't captured by the official ledger — appears in **environmental accounting**, where unpaid ecosystem services (clean water, pollination) create real value that standard GDP ignores until someone builds a framework to price it. It also echoes **unpaid household and care work**: labor that is enormously productive but sits outside the tax-and-monitor boundary, so it vanishes from national accounts the same way informal trade does. In both cases, the fix isn't to make the activity disappear — it's to decide whether and how to count it.

## A real-world case
In 2014, a number of European countries formally changed their GDP calculations to include prostitution and narcotics sales in their official GDP statistics, in line with international accounting standards, prompting an increase between 3–7% [1]. Nothing about the underlying activity necessarily changed on that date — what changed was the decision to count it. This is a clean demonstration that measured GDP is a product of accounting rules as much as of production. (For contrast, Italy's inclusion of informal activity since 1987 added an estimated 18% [1].)

## Second-order effects
If a country counts more of its informal activity, its measured GDP rises without any new goods being produced [1]. Illustrative: this can flatter growth figures, shift its relative ranking, and even change its debt-to-GDP ratio and borrowing costs, purely through reclassification. In China, where approximately half of urban workers belonged to the informal economy as of 2004 [2], the informal sector is described as fast-growing, dynamic, and highly competitive, contributing substantially to growth [2] — yet it operates alongside a formal private sector contributing roughly 60% of GDP and 80% of urban employment [3]. That overlap means policies aimed at the formal economy can ripple into the informal one, and vice versa. One plausible downstream effect: as the state's monitoring capacity grows, activity migrates between the two categories, so "shrinking the informal sector" may partly mean relabeling rather than transforming.

## A question to sit with
If informal activity is real enough to employ half a country's urban workers but invisible enough to be excluded from its GDP, then what does it actually mean to say a country's economy is "growing"?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **informal economy** — (noun phrase, /ɪnˈfɔːrməl ɪˈkɒnəmi/) — the part of an economy neither taxed nor monitored by government. _Example: The informal economy provides work that never appears in official statistics._
2. **grey market** — (noun, /ɡreɪ ˈmɑːrkɪt/) — trade in goods or labor outside official channels but not necessarily illegal. _Example: The informal sector can be described as a grey market in labour._
3. **stigmatize** — (verb, /ˈstɪɡmətaɪz/) — to treat something as shameful or disreputable. _Example: The informal sector is sometimes stigmatized as troublesome and unmanageable._
4. **gross domestic product (GDP)** — (noun, /ɡroʊs dəˈmɛstɪk ˈprɒdʌkt/) — the total value of goods and services a country produces. _Example: Informal activity is often excluded from a country's GDP._
5. **unitary construct** — (noun phrase, /ˈjuːnɪtɛri ˈkɒnstrʌkt/) — a single, agreed-upon concept. _Example: Researchers note that definitions of the informal economy fail to converge around a unitary construct._
6. **linkage** — (noun, /ˈlɪŋkɪdʒ/) — a connection or relationship between two things. _Example: The paper examines the linkages between the informal and formal economies._
7. **alleviate** — (verb, /əˈliːvieɪt/) — to make a problem less severe. _Example: Some local governments encourage the informal economy to alleviate unemployment._
8. **regulatory environment** — (noun phrase, /ˈrɛɡjələtɔːri ɪnˈvaɪrənmənt/) — the system of rules governing an activity. _Example: Inclusive policy and a supportive regulatory environment can promote equitable linkages._

11. **purchasing power parity (PPP)** — (noun phrase, /ˈpɜːrtʃəsɪŋ ˈpaʊər ˈpærɪti/) — a measure comparing economies by what money actually buys. _Example: Measured by purchasing power parity, China has been the world's largest economy since 2016._
12. **bottom of the economic pyramid** — (noun phrase, /ˈbɒtəm əv ði ˌiːkəˈnɒmɪk ˈpɪrəmɪd/) — the poorest segment of a population, viewed as a market. _Example: Management researchers study the informal sector from poverty alleviation at the bottom of the economic pyramid to entrepreneurship._
