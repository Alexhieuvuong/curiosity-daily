# When Money Wasn't a Thing: The African Pre-Colonial Paradox

> What happens when economists' tidy definitions of money collide with the messy reality of how people actually exchanged value before colonial coinage?

## Why this is interesting
The standard story of money's evolution—from barter to commodity money to fiat currency—is so ingrained that we rarely question it. But a 1959 study of pre-colonial African economies reveals a startling paradox: economists who demand multiple functions from money in modern contexts suddenly accept a single function as proof of "real" money when studying ancient or non-Western societies [5]. This double standard exposes how our understanding of money is shaped more by cultural bias than by consistent theory.

## First principles
Money is fundamentally a social technology for solving the problem of indirect exchange. Instead of trading goods directly (barter), money allows you to sell to one person and buy from another later. Economists identify four primary functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value, and standard of deferred payment [2]. A cowrie shell or gold coin that serves all four is clearly money. But what about objects that only serve one or two? The 1959 study shows that when economists encounter societies where cattle serve as a store of value but not a medium of exchange, or where salt bars are used only for large transactions, they often declare these "primitive money" [5]. This is not a neutral observation—it's a definitional trick that imposes Western categories onto different economic logics.

## Break it into pieces
- **What counts as money?** The four-function definition is a modern, Western construct. Pre-colonial African economies used diverse objects—cowries, iron bars, cattle, cloth—that fulfilled different subsets of these functions depending on context [5].
- **Why do economists change the rules?** The same scholars who demand all four functions for modern money suddenly accept one function (e.g., store of value) as sufficient when studying ancient societies. This inconsistency reveals a colonial mindset that treats non-Western economies as "simpler" or "earlier" stages of development [5].
- **What was actually happening?** Many African societies had complex systems of credit, gift exchange, and social obligation that didn't require a universal medium of exchange. The absence of coinage didn't mean the absence of sophisticated economic coordination.
- **Who benefits from the definition?** Defining non-Western exchange systems as "not money" justifies colonial intervention—the idea that "real" money (coinage) must be introduced to "civilize" these economies. The definition itself becomes a tool of power.
- **What about the "barter myth"?** The idea that money emerged to replace barter is itself contested. Anthropologists have shown that barter is rare in stateless societies; gift exchange and credit networks were more common. The African case further undermines this origin story.

## Follow the incentives
European colonial administrations and trading companies had a clear incentive to impose coinage on African economies: it made taxation, accounting, and long-distance trade vastly easier. A standardized currency that could be counted, stored, and transferred replaced messy systems of social obligation and seasonal exchange. The colonial state profited by issuing coinage and taxing in that coinage, forcing Africans into wage labor to earn it [5]. Meanwhile, economists writing from European universities had an intellectual incentive to see non-Western economies as "primitive" stages of development—this justified both academic hierarchies and colonial policy. The 1959 paper explicitly criticizes this "astounding fact" that scholars apply different standards to different societies [5].

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same pattern appears in anthropology's long debate over "reciprocity" versus "market exchange." In many Indigenous societies, gift-giving creates social bonds that cannot be reduced to economic calculation. Western observers often dismiss these as "not real economics" because they don't fit the utility-maximizing model. Similarly, the modern informal economy—street vendors, barter networks, mutual aid—is often treated as "not real money" by official statistics, even though it represents a massive share of global economic activity. The definitional boundary between "money" and "not money" is always a political choice, not a neutral description.

## A real-world case
The 1959 study examines claims that "money was to be found in much of the African continent before the impact of the European world" [5]. It finds that these claims "tend to evaporate or to emerge as tricks of definition." For example, cowrie shells were widely used in West Africa for small transactions and bride-price payments. They served as a medium of exchange and unit of account, but their value fluctuated wildly with supply from the Indian Ocean trade. Were they "money"? By a strict four-function definition, no—they were poor stores of value. But by the looser definition economists applied to ancient societies, yes. The paper's key insight is that the question itself is rigged: the definition changes depending on whether the observer wants to include or exclude the case.

## Second-order effects
If we accept that pre-colonial African economies had sophisticated non-monetary exchange systems, several consequences follow. First, the standard narrative of economic "development" as a linear march from barter to money to banking collapses—it was never a universal path. Second, it undermines the idea that Western-style monetary systems are "natural" or "inevitable"; they are historically contingent institutions imposed through colonialism. Third, it raises uncomfortable questions about modern monetary policy: if money's value is purely social convention (as fiat money demonstrates [2]), then why do we treat non-Western systems as "less real"? Finally, it suggests that the informal economies of today—crypto, local currencies, time banks—are not aberrations but continuations of a much older human pattern of creating diverse exchange systems tailored to specific social contexts.

## A question to sit with
If money is ultimately a social convention, what gives the modern fiat dollar more "reality" than the cowrie shell—other than the coercive power of the state that enforces its acceptance?
