# Why Top Venture Capitalists Get a Discount on Startup Equity

> The most sought-after venture capitalists can acquire startup equity at a 10–14% discount, revealing the hidden value of reputation [5].

## Why this is interesting
When a startup has multiple financing offers, it consistently chooses the offer from a high-reputation venture capital firm—even when that offer is 10–14% *cheaper* for the VC [5]. This counterintuitive behavior reveals that venture capital is not just about money; it's about buying access to a network, expertise, and a brand that signals quality to everyone else.

## First principles
Venture capital is a form of private equity financing where firms invest in early-stage, high-growth startups in exchange for equity [1]. Startups face extreme uncertainty—most fail—so VCs take on high risk in hopes of outsized returns [1]. The core mechanic is simple: money goes in, equity comes out. But the twist is that money is not the only thing VCs provide. They also offer "extra-financial" services: strategic advice, introductions to customers and partners, help with hiring, and a reputation that signals to other investors that the startup is worth backing [1][5]. A startup's choice of VC is therefore a bet on both capital and credibility.

## Break it into pieces
- **Why would a startup accept less money for the same equity?** Because the VC's reputation acts as a certification that the startup is legitimate and promising, making it easier to raise future rounds and attract talent.
- **What is the "discount" actually measuring?** It's the price the startup pays for the VC's extra-financial value—services that are functionally distinct from the cash itself [5].
- **How do VCs compete for deals?** When multiple VCs offer financing, the startup doesn't just pick the highest valuation; it weighs the VC's track record, network, and ability to add value.
- **Why do high-reputation VCs get a discount?** Because their brand is so valuable that startups are willing to give up more ownership (i.e., accept a lower valuation) to secure their backing.
- **What happens to the discount over time?** Illustrative: As the VC's reputation grows, the discount may widen, creating a virtuous cycle where the most successful VCs get the best deals, reinforcing their advantage.

## Follow the incentives
- **Entrepreneurs** want not just cash but also credibility, access to networks, and strategic guidance. They are willing to pay a premium (in the form of a lower valuation) for a top-tier VC's stamp of approval [5].
- **High-reputation VCs** have built their brand through past successes. They can leverage this reputation to negotiate better terms (a lower price per share) because startups compete to have them as investors [5].
- **Lower-reputation VCs** Illustrative: must offer higher valuations or better terms to win deals, but they lack the signaling power of top firms.
- **The startup's board and future investors** see the VC's involvement as a signal of quality, reducing information asymmetry and making it easier to raise subsequent rounds [5].

## How it echoes elsewhere
- **In academia**, Illustrative: a famous professor's name on a paper increases its citation count and the likelihood of publication, even if the professor contributed little. The reputation of the author certifies the work's quality, much like a top VC certifies a startup.
- **In luxury goods**, Illustrative: a brand like Hermès charges a premium not just for the product but for the status it confers. The startup's "discount" is the inverse: the startup pays *less* cash but gives up more equity to buy the VC's brand.

## A real-world case
The first modern venture capital firm, American Research & Development Corporation (ARDC), invested $70,000 in Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) in 1957—a bet that yielded a return of over $355 million and is considered the first "unicorn"-level return [3]. ARDC's lead board member, Dorothy Rowe, provided not just capital but strategic oversight. While the exact discount is not recorded, the case illustrates how early VCs built reputations through a single defining success, creating the template for the reputation-driven dynamics that persist today [3].

## Second-order effects
- **Concentration of power**: Top VCs get better deals, which leads to better returns, which attracts more capital, which lets them offer even more value—creating a winner-take-most dynamic in the VC industry.
- **Homogenization of startups**: If all the best startups are backed by the same handful of VCs, those VCs' preferences (e.g., for certain business models or growth strategies) may shape entire industries.
- **Barriers to entry**: New VCs struggle to compete because they lack the reputation to win top deals, even if they have deep pockets. This can reduce diversity of thought in startup funding.
- **Misaligned incentives**: Entrepreneurs may overpay for reputation, accepting a lower valuation than they could get from a less-known VC, if the top VC's involvement later proves less valuable than expected.

## A question to sit with
If the discount for reputation is rational, what happens when a VC's reputation is based on past success that may not predict future performance? Can a top VC coast on its brand, extracting value from startups without actually adding proportional value?
