# Why the First Unicorn Was a $70,000 Bet on a Computer

> Before “unicorn” was a buzzword, a $70,000 check in 1957 quietly created the template for every venture capital firm that followed.

## Why this is interesting
We treat unicorns—startups valued over $1 billion—as a modern phenomenon, but the first one predates the term by over half a century. The story of American Research & Development Corporation (ARDC) and Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) shows that the core logic of venture capital—high risk, equity for control, and a single breakout hit—hasn’t changed since 1946. What has changed is the scale, the vocabulary, and the sheer number of players chasing the same myth.

## First principles
Venture capital is a form of private equity financing given to startups with high growth potential, in exchange for an ownership stake [1]. Because startups face high uncertainty, most VC investments fail; the model only works if a few successes pay for many failures [1]. The first modern venture capital firm, ARDC, was founded in 1946, and its 1957 investment of $70,000 in DEC became the defining success of early venture capital—the first “unicorn”-level return [3]. That single bet established the template: take a big risk on an unproven technology, accept that most bets will fail, and rely on one massive winner to justify the whole portfolio.

## Break it into pieces
- **Why did ARDC exist?** ARDC, founded in 1946, is considered the first modern venture capital firm [3].
- **What made DEC special?** ARDC's first breakout investment was in DEC in 1957, which became the defining success of early venture capital [3].
- **Why is the $70,000 figure so striking?** It’s tiny by today’s standards, yet it produced the first unicorn-level return, showing that the *structure* of the bet matters more than the size [3].
- **How does the term “unicorn” fit?** Aileen Lee coined it in 2013 to describe the statistical rarity of $1 billion+ private startups—a label that turned a business phenomenon into a cultural myth [3].
- **What’s the difference between then and now?** ARDC's DEC investment was its first breakout; modern firms like Antler made 443 deals in a single year (2024), spreading risk far thinner [2][3].

## Follow the incentives
Venture capitalists take on high risk in exchange for significant control and ownership [1]. ARDC’s incentive was clear: a few wins must outweigh many losses, and DEC was the win that made the model viable [3]. Modern VCs face the same incentive structure, but with more pressure to show activity—Antler topped PitchBook’s “Most Active Venture Capital Globally” in 2024 with 443 deals [2]. The incentive to *do many deals* can conflict with the incentive to *pick winners*, because reputation and deal flow often matter more than precision. Meanwhile, entrepreneurs accept equity dilution because VC money comes with strategic advice and credibility—high-reputation VCs are three times more likely to have their offers accepted, even though they acquire equity at a 10–14% discount [5].

## How it echoes elsewhere
Illustrative: The same “one big hit pays for many misses” logic appears in **pharmaceutical R&D**, where most drug candidates fail in trials but a single blockbuster can fund decades of research. It also appears in **oil exploration**, where dry wells are common but one gusher justifies the whole drilling program. In both fields, the key is not avoiding failure but structuring the portfolio so that a rare success is large enough to compensate.

## A real-world case
The ARDC–DEC case is the canonical example [3]. ARDC invested $70,000 in DEC in 1957, with Dorothy Rowe as the lead board member [3]. This investment became the first unicorn-level return, proving that early-stage institutional venture capital could work [3]. It wasn’t just about the money—it was about the *structure*: a dedicated firm, an equity stake, and a board seat. That combination—not the size of the check—became the blueprint for the entire industry.

## Second-order effects
The ARDC success didn’t just create a template; it created an *expectation*. Once a $70,000 bet could produce a unicorn, every subsequent firm tried to replicate the formula, leading to more capital chasing earlier-stage, riskier deals. This has a compounding effect: as more money enters, valuations rise, which makes the “unicorn” label more common—by May 2024 there were 1,248 unicorns worldwide [1][3]. But the label’s inflation may be a warning: if everyone can be a unicorn, the term loses its original meaning of statistical rarity. The 2022 valuation drops—driven by higher interest rates and regulatory scrutiny—show that the myth can deflate as fast as it inflates [3].

## A question to sit with
If the first unicorn was built on a single $70,000 bet, what does it say about the modern industry’s reliance on hundreds of deals and billions of dollars—are we more efficient, or just more diluted?
