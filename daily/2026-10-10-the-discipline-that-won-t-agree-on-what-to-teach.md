# The Discipline That Won't Agree on What to Teach

> Every other field of economics hands its students a canon. Development economics hands them an argument.

## Why this is interesting
Most academic disciplines converge on a core curriculum: a set of theories, methods, and settled findings that every graduate is expected to know. Development economics, by contrast, is explicitly described as having "no consensus on what students should know" [1]. That is not a bug in the field's organization — it reflects a genuine, unresolved dispute about what causes some countries to grow rich while others stay poor, and whether outsiders can do anything about it.

## First principles
Start with the object of study. Economics is a social science examining the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services, and the behaviour of economic agents — households, firms, buyers, sellers [2]. Development economics narrows that lens to low- and middle-income countries, asking not just how to promote growth and structural change, but how to improve the potential of the mass of the population through health, education, and workplace conditions [1]. The irreducible mechanic is this: the field must explain *change over time* in whole societies, where the variables (institutions, culture, politics) are entangled and rarely controllable. That makes clean, replicable theory hard — and disagreement almost inevitable.

## Break it into pieces
- **Convergence or non-convergence?** The field explicitly studies why households, regions, and countries converge toward rich-country living standards — or fail to [1]. If the answer were settled, so would the curriculum be.
- **Means versus ends.** Is economic growth the goal, or merely a means? Human development thinking insists growth "is only a means of enlarging people's choices" [3].
- **Market incentives versus dirigisme.** Should policy restructure market incentives, or should the state direct the process? This is the fault line running through the field's history [4].
- **Shock therapy versus gradualism.** Even when the goal is agreed, the *path* — fast liberalization or paced, sequenced reform — is contested [5].
- **What counts as progress?** Income growth alone, or living conditions, freedoms, and political institutions [3]?

## Follow the incentives
Who profits from which answer? International organisations like the United Nations and the World Bank adopted human development as a holistic evaluation framework [3] — a measurement approach that expands what counts as success and, implicitly, what they are accountable for. On the other side, critics argued that the "dirigiste dogma" — state-directed development — was itself a cause of poor outcomes, and that its demise would benefit "both economics and the economies of developing countries" [4]. Notice the incentive structure: a discipline with no fixed canon cannot easily certify expertise, so authority flows instead to whoever can claim the most persuasive narrative about *why* nations succeed. That rewards confident frameworks over settled knowledge.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same pattern — a field whose object of study resists controlled experiment — appears in **macroeconomics**, which analyses economies as interacting systems of production, savings, investment, and policy [2]. Illustrative: it also echoes in **medicine's** debates over complex, multi-causal conditions: when you cannot isolate variables, you get competing schools rather than one textbook. The deeper mechanic is that **causal inference in open systems is hard**, and disciplines that face it stay argumentative.

## A real-world case
The intellectual history of the "dirigiste dogma" is a concrete case. A classic critique, first published in 1983 and revised in 1997, attacked the state-directed development economics dominant in the early 1980s; the author concluded its demise would benefit both economics and developing economies, and the following decades appeared to vindicate that critique — yet he warned that dirigiste tendencies had not vanished but were "emerging in new guises" [4]. This is the field arguing with itself in real time, and the argument never fully closes.

## Second-order effects
If a discipline has no agreed canon, several things follow. First, **policy advice becomes path-dependent on which school holds sway** — Illustrative: the same country can receive opposite recommendations a decade apart. Second, **the field's credibility is fragile**: Illustrative: outsiders may read internal disagreement as incompetence rather than as honest uncertainty. Third, as the 2004 critique notes, the global financial crisis cast doubt on the neoclassical paradigm even in advanced economies, undermining the "American style capitalism" model that developing countries were once urged to imitate [5]. My analysis: when the model's home country stumbles, the export version loses its authority — which may reopen space for the very dirigiste ideas supposedly retired [4].

## A question to sit with
If development economics has no consensus on what to teach, is that a sign the field is immature — or a sign it is the only honest one, because its subject genuinely resists a single answer?

## Go deeper
- Read the two critiques side by side: the 1985 attack on dirigisme [4] and the 2004 call to rethink development economics after the global financial crisis [5]. Where do they agree, and where do they simply swap which orthodoxy they distrust?
- Trace how the shift from "economic development" to "human development" changed what governments and international bodies measure [3] — and who became accountable for what.
- Ask whether "no consensus on what students should know" [1] is unique to development economics or common to any field studying open, complex systems [2].

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **canon** — (noun, /ˈkænən/) — the body of works or ideas accepted as authoritative in a field. _Example: Development economics famously lacks a settled canon._
2. **dirigiste** — (adjective, /ˌdɪrɪˈʒiːst/) — favouring strong state direction of the economy. _Example: The dirigiste dogma assumed the state could steer industrialization._
3. **convergence** — (noun, /kənˈvɜːrdʒəns/) — the process of poorer economies catching up to richer ones. _Example: The field studies why convergence happens for some regions but not others._
4. **heterodox** — (adjective, /ˈhetərədɒks/) — not conforming to established or orthodox theory. _Example: Heterodox approaches often inform development policy._
5. **intertemporal** — (adjective, /ˌɪntərˈtempərəl/) — spanning or relating to different points in time. _Example: Intertemporal optimization is used to analyse development projects._
6. **holistic** — (adjective, /hoʊˈlɪstɪk/) — considering a whole system rather than isolated parts. _Example: Human development takes a holistic view of a country's progress._
7. **paradigm** — (noun, /ˈpærədaɪm/) — a dominant framework of assumptions and methods. _Example: The financial crisis shook confidence in the neoclassical paradigm._
8. **gradualist** — (adjective, /ˈɡrædʒuəlɪst/) — favouring slow, step-by-step change. _Example: Gradualists emphasized pacing and sequencing over shock therapy._
9. **structural change** — (noun phrase, /ˈstrʌktʃərəl tʃeɪndʒ/) — a shift in how an economy's activity is organized. _Example: Development economics studies growth alongside structural change._
10. **normative** — (adjective, /ˈnɔːrmətɪv/) — prescribing what ought to be, rather than describing what is. _Example: Debates over development goals are inherently normative._
11. **vindicate** — (verb, /ˈvɪndɪkeɪt/) — to prove right or justified. _Example: Later decades appeared to vindicate the critique of dirigisme._
12. **entangled** — (adjective, /ɪnˈtæŋɡəld/) — deeply intertwined and hard to separate. _Example: Institutions, culture, and politics are entangled variables in development._
