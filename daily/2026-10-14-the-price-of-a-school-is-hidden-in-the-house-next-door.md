# The Price of a School Is Hidden in the House Next Door

> You never buy a school. You buy a house — and the school's quality is quietly folded into the receipt.

## Why this is interesting
When a house sells, the buyer is paying for walls, a roof, and a location — but also for things that aren't in the deed at all: the quality of the nearby school, the cost of heating it, the commute that hasn't been built yet. Economists call this "capitalization," and it turns ordinary home prices into a measuring stick for public goods that no one ever prices directly [5]. It's a neat trick: the market does the valuation that governments, voters, and school boards argue about endlessly.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: a house is fixed in space, so whatever sits near it — a good school, a bad commute, a cheaper heating system — attaches to that house and can't be unbundled from it [5]. Because the house is immovable, buyers who want the nearby amenity must pay for it through the house price. Researchers use "hedonic price estimation" to tease apart how much of a sale price reflects each attribute, including school quality, energy efficiency, and future transportation improvements [5]. In other words, the price of a home is a bundle, and the bundle includes things you'd never find on a listing sheet [5].

## Break it into pieces
- **What exactly is being priced?** Is the school's test-score premium about teaching quality, or about the socioeconomic background of the pupils who attend [5]?
- **Why does the same school cost more in some places than others?** The effect appears to hinge on how easily new housing can be built nearby [5].
- **Can you separate the amenity from the house?** If the school improves, does the price rise — or was the price already anticipating it?
- **Who captures the gain?** The homeowner, the seller, the developer, or the school district?
- **What happens when the amenity is a promise, not a fact?** Future transportation is priced before it exists [5].

## Follow the incentives
Homebuyers pay for school quality through the purchase price, and the study finds that a one-standard-deviation increase in average test scores raises prices by about 3 percent on average — comparable to findings in the U.K. and the U.S. [5]. Crucially, that premium seems tied to pupils' socioeconomic background rather than school effectiveness [5], which means buyers may be paying for *who attends*, not *how well it teaches*. The premium is largest where housing supply is inelastic — where new homes are hard to build — because competition for a fixed stock of houses bids the price up [5]. On the heating side, the estimated price discount for electric heating is 5–6 percent of the house value [5]. So sellers of well-located, efficiently heated homes profit; buyers bear the cost; and the mechanism rewards whoever already owns the scarce thing.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same "immovable asset absorbs nearby value" logic shows up in **filtering**: when supply is sufficient, a housing unit becomes more affordable with age as it depreciates and passes to lower-income occupants, but when supply is insufficient, reverse filtering (gentrification) pushes prices *up* and displaces lower-income residents [2]. Capitalization and filtering are two faces of one coin — both are about how a fixed stock of housing sorts people by what they can pay. The pattern also echoes in **real estate economics** broadly, which applies supply-and-demand analysis to describe and predict these price patterns [3].

## A real-world case
The Helsinki Metropolitan Area study in Finland used house prices and hedonic estimation to test whether school quality is capitalized into prices, finding the ~3 percent premium per standard deviation of test scores, concentrated in areas with inelastic land supply [5]. The same research examined how heating technologies are locked into houses at construction time, letting the authors isolate the stand-alone value of a cost-saving technology — finding a 5–6 percent discount for electric heating [5]. Both results come from the same research program and method, showing that a single house price encodes multiple hidden valuations at once.

## Second-order effects
If school quality is capitalized into house prices, then improving a school doesn't just help students — it hands a windfall to existing homeowners and raises the barrier for newcomers [5]. That creates a perverse incentive: homeowners may resist new housing precisely because scarcity keeps their capitalized premium high [5]. And if the premium tracks pupils' socioeconomic background rather than school effectiveness [5], then buyers are effectively purchasing access to a peer group, not instruction — which could entrench segregation even as schools "improve." Meanwhile, the heating-technology finding [5] suggests that building codes and construction-time choices silently redistribute value for decades, long after the builder is gone.

## A question to sit with
If a school's value is already priced into the houses around it, does improving the school actually help the children — or mostly the people who already own the homes?

## Go deeper
- How do researchers statistically separate a school's effect from everything else nearby? Look into "boundary discontinuity" designs [5].
- Compare the Finnish findings to the U.K. and U.S. results the study cites — why might the magnitudes converge across very different housing systems [5]?
- Explore the link between capitalization and filtering: when does a price premium signal quality, and when does it signal scarcity [2][5]?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **capitalization** — (noun, /ˌkæpɪtəlaɪˈzeɪʃən/) — the process by which the value of an amenity gets absorbed into the price of an asset. _Example: School quality capitalization means a good school raises nearby house prices._
2. **hedonic** — (adjective, /hɪˈdɒnɪk/) — relating to the estimated value of a good's individual attributes. _Example: Hedonic price estimation breaks a house price into its component features._
3. **inelastic** — (adjective, /ˌɪnɪˈlæstɪk/) — not responsive to changes in price or supply; slow to adjust. _Example: In an inelastic housing market, prices rise sharply when demand grows._
4. **discontinuity** — (noun, /ˌdɪskɒntɪˈnjuːɪti/) — a break or gap; in research, a sharp boundary used to compare otherwise similar areas. _Example: The boundary discontinuity design compares houses on either side of a school district line._
5. **amenity** — (noun, /əˈmiːnɪti/) — a feature or facility that adds comfort or value to a location. _Example: A nearby park is an amenity that buyers will pay to live beside._
6. **premium** — (noun, /ˈpriːmiəm/) — an extra amount paid above a baseline price. _Example: Buyers pay a premium for homes zoned to high-scoring schools._
7. **filtering** — (noun, /ˈfɪltərɪŋ/) — the process by which housing becomes more affordable as it ages and passes to lower-income occupants. _Example: Filtering depends on enough new construction to keep supply flowing._
8. **gentrification** — (noun, /ˌdʒɛntrɪfɪˈkeɪʃən/) — the process by which a lower-income area becomes occupied by higher-income residents, raising prices. _Example: Reverse filtering is another name for gentrification in tight markets._
9. **depreciation** — (noun, /dɪˌpriːʃiˈeɪʃən/) — a decline in value over time. _Example: A home's depreciation is what allows it to filter down to cheaper price tiers._
10. **monocentric** — (adjective, /ˌmɒnoʊˈsɛntrɪk/) — organized around a single central point, such as one downtown. _Example: The monocentric city model assumes jobs cluster in one central business district._
11. **polycentric** — (adjective, /ˌpɒliˈsɛntrɪk/) — having multiple centers rather than one. _Example: Edge cities reflect a shift toward polycentric urban structure._
12. **agglomeration** — (noun, /əˌɡlɒməˈreɪʃən/) — the clustering of people or firms that creates economic benefits. _Example: Economies of agglomeration explain why firms cluster in dense districts._
