# The Vanishing Middle: Why "Conduit" Havens Move Value They Never Keep

> Most of the world's offshore money isn't hiding in a Caribbean vault — it's passing through a respectable European office park on its way somewhere else.

## Why this is interesting
We picture tax havens as destinations — tropical islands where money goes to disappear. But research now splits the offshore world into two roles: "sink" jurisdictions where value piles up, and "conduit" jurisdictions through which value *moves* toward those sinks [1]. The twist is that the biggest conduits are often wealthy, respectable, onshore-looking countries — and the money is often just passing through, never staying [1][2]. Understanding the difference changes who you think the villain is.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: a multinational wants to move profit from a high-tax country to a low-tax one. It can't teleport the money, so it routes it through intermediate legal structures. A "sink" OFC is where a disproportionate amount of value *disappears* from the economic system; a "conduit" OFC is where a disproportionate amount of value *moves toward* the sinks [1]. The conduit doesn't keep the money — it's a hallway, not a room. Crucially, a jurisdiction's headline tax rate tells you almost nothing: modern corporate havens have non-zero official rates and high OECD compliance, yet use base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) tools to push *effective* tax rates toward zero [2][3]. So the real question isn't "how low is the rate?" but "what does this place *do* to money passing through?"

## Break it into pieces
- **Sink vs. conduit:** which jurisdictions accumulate value, and which merely route it? [1]
- **Headline vs. effective rate:** why a 12.5% or 25% official rate can coexist with a near-zero effective rate [3].
- **The treaty paradox:** traditional zero-rate havens have *few* bilateral tax treaties; modern corporate havens have *large* treaty networks — why? [2]
- **Respectability as strategy:** why havens market themselves as "knowledge economies" and require "substantive presence" [3].
- **Where the loss actually lands:** who bears the cost of the tax that never gets paid [1][2].

## Follow the incentives
The conduit jurisdiction profits not by taxing flows but by *attracting* them — promoting itself as a "knowledge economy" and maintaining respectability so corporations will use it as a regional headquarters and so it can access bilateral tax treaties [3]. To gain access to bilateral tax treaties (which let corporations claim benefits), a haven must appear respectable — so it keeps a non-zero headline rate and demands "substantive presence," roughly an "employment tax" of about 2–3% of profits shielded [3]. The multinational profits by achieving a near-zero effective rate across *all* countries it has treaties with, not just the haven [2]. The countries losing revenue are those that are not tax havens — they bear the loss of tax revenues [2]. Each actor is responding rationally to the rules as written; the friction is structural, not personal.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The conduit mechanic is really about **intermediation** — value passing through a node that captures a fee without owning the asset. Illustrative: the same pattern appears in payment processing (money flows through a processor that never holds it) and in supply-chain "pass-through" distributors who add margin without manufacturing. In each case, the intermediary's incentive is to maximize *volume through the pipe*, not to hold the goods — which is exactly why regulating the endpoint alone misses the hallway.

## A real-world case
The 2017 research split the offshore world into 24 "Sink" OFCs and five "Conduit" OFCs [1]. The conduits are frequently the respectable names: the Netherlands, the U.K., Switzerland, Ireland, and Singapore rank as key corporate tax havens in CORPNET's "Orbis connections," while Zucman's "quantum of funds" ranks Ireland as the largest global corporate tax haven [3]. Ireland's "double Irish" BEPS tool is credited with the largest build-up of untaxed corporate offshore cash in history [3]. Note the paradox: Ireland's headline rate is 12.5% — not zero — yet it functions as a premier conduit [3]. That is the whole point: the rate is a facade; the routing is the mechanism.

## Second-order effects
If conduits are the real chokepoints, then transparency rules aimed at *sinks* (islands, secrecy) may miss most of the flow. The sources note that major *onshore* international financial centres — not offshore ones — had become the dominant locations for corporate tax avoidance schemes, costing an estimated US$200 billion in lost annual tax revenue [1]. My read: this suggests enforcement that targets headline rates or tropical secrecy will keep chasing the wrong map, while treaty networks and "substance" requirements become the true levers. There's also a reputational feedback loop — jurisdictions that adopt strict economic-substance rules may end up *ahead* of onshore regimes [3], turning compliance itself into a competitive advantage.

## A question to sit with
If a conduit's entire value is that money passes through without stopping, is it more honest to tax the flow at the pipe — or to accept that the pipe will always exist and focus only on the endpoints?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **conduit** — (noun, /ˈkɒndjuɪt/) — a channel through which something passes. _Example: A conduit haven moves value toward sink havens without keeping it._
2. **incommensurate** — (adjective, /ˌɪnkəˈmɛnʃərət/) — out of proportion; not matching in scale. _Example: An OFC hosts financial services incommensurate with its tiny domestic economy._
3. **pejorative** — (adjective, /pɪˈdʒɒrətɪv/) — expressing disapproval or contempt. _Example: "Tax haven" is often used as a pejorative label._
4. **base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS)** — (noun phrase, /beɪs ɪˈrəʊʒən/) — strategies that move profits to low-tax jurisdictions to shrink the tax base elsewhere. _Example: BEPS tools let corporations push effective rates toward zero._
5. **effective tax rate (ETR)** — (noun phrase, /ɪˈfɛktɪv tæks reɪt/) — the actual rate paid after deductions and schemes, versus the headline rate. _Example: Ireland's ETR for some multinationals is far below its 12.5% headline._
6. **tax-neutral** — (adjective, /tæks ˈnjuːtrəl/) — describing a structure that neither adds nor removes tax on flows passing through. _Example: The Cayman exempted company is a tax-neutral vehicle._
7. **withholding tax** — (noun, /wɪðˈhəʊldɪŋ tæks/) — tax deducted at source on payments like dividends or interest. _Example: Tax-neutral structures often levy no withholding tax on outflows._
8. **substantive presence** — (noun phrase, /səbˈstæntɪv ˈprɛzəns/) — a real operational footprint (staff, offices) required to claim treaty benefits. _Example: Ireland requires substantive presence to access its treaty network._
9. **bilateral tax treaty** — (noun phrase, /baɪˈlætərəl tæks ˈtriːti/) — an agreement between two countries on how income is taxed. _Example: Modern havens build large bilateral tax treaty networks._
10. **frictioneering** — (noun, /ˌfrɪkʃəˈnɪərɪŋ/) — the art of exploiting economic friction between jurisdictions. _Example: The academic study frames offshore states as practicing "frictioneering."_
11. **secrecy jurisdiction** — (noun phrase, /ˈsiːkrəsi ˈdʒʊərɪsdɪkʃən/) — a place that shields financial information from outside authorities. _Example: The Financial Secrecy Index ranks secrecy jurisdictions._
12. **disproportionate** — (adjective, /ˌdɪsprəˈpɔːʃənət/) — far larger or smaller than expected relative to the whole. _Example: Sink havens attract a disproportionate share of global value._
