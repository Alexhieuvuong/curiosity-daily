# Why a Half-Built Tower Is a Risk That Refuses to Be Priced

> Insurance works because most risks are stable — but a building under construction changes its own risk profile every single day.

## Why this is interesting
Builder’s risk insurance covers a structure that is, by definition, incomplete and constantly changing. That makes it a fascinating edge case: the thing being insured is not a fixed object but a process. Pricing that process requires predicting not just *if* a loss happens, but *when* in the construction timeline it happens — because the same building is a different risk at each stage.

## First principles
Insurance is a transfer of financial risk: a policyholder pays a known, small premium to an insurer, who promises to compensate for a larger, uncertain loss [2]. For this to work, the insurer must estimate the probability and size of that loss. Most insurance — say, a home or a car — covers an asset whose risk profile is relatively stable from day to day. Builder’s risk insurance, by contrast, covers “materials, fixtures and/or equipment being used in the construction or renovation of a building” [1]. The insured object is not a finished building; it is a building *becoming* one. Every day, new materials arrive, new systems are installed, and new hazards appear — the risk profile shifts as construction progresses. The risk is not a single number; it is a curve that shifts as construction progresses.

## Break it into pieces
- **What exactly is covered?** Builder’s risk protects the insurable interest in materials, fixtures, and equipment on-site, not the finished structure itself [1]. That means the policy must define *when* something counts as part of the building versus a loose material.
- **Who is the insured?** Multiple parties — owner, contractor, subcontractors — may each hold an insurable interest in the same project [1]. A single policy must somehow cover overlapping interests without double-paying.
- **What is the loss event?** The policy covers “physical loss or damage from a covered cause” [1]. But a half-built building has causes of loss that a finished one lacks — the structure is exposed and incomplete, creating unique vulnerabilities.
- **How is the premium set?** Unlike a finished building, whose risk can be assessed once, a construction site’s risk changes weekly. The underwriter must price a moving target [3].
- **What happens when the risk is too big?** The insurer can offload part of the risk to another insurer via reinsurance [2]. But for a massive project, even that may not be enough — the risk is concentrated in time and place.

## Follow the incentives
The policyholder wants the cheapest premium that covers the full project [1]. The insurer wants a premium that reflects the *peak* risk, not the average — because a single loss during the most hazardous phase can be catastrophic. The contractor has an incentive to rush, because time is money, but rushing increases the chance of a loss. The insurer, therefore, has an incentive to impose conditions on the policyholder. The ultimate bearer of the risk is the reinsurer, who takes on the portion the primary insurer deems too large [2]. Each layer of the chain pushes risk downward to whoever is best positioned to *prevent* the loss — or least able to refuse it.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same “moving target” problem appears in other domains where the insured object changes over time. A network today is not the network tomorrow, just as a building at foundation stage is not the building at roof stage. Both require underwriters to price a process, not an asset. The pattern also appears in other fields where risk profiles shift as a project progresses — the “building” is the project itself, and the “construction” is the process.

## A real-world case
Illustrative: A contractor insures a 20-story office tower under a builder’s risk policy. At the framing stage, the building is an open skeleton: no fire walls, no sprinklers, no exterior cladding. A spark from a welder ignites wooden formwork, and the fire spreads through the exposed structure before firefighters can reach the upper floors. The loss is enormous — far larger than the premium paid. The insurer pays, then raises the premium for the contractor’s next project, and requires a full-time fire watch during any hot work. The lesson: the policy priced the *average* risk of the project, but the loss happened at the *peak* risk moment. This is a simplified scenario, not a verified case, but it captures the core tension.

## Second-order effects
If builder’s risk premiums are set based on the riskiest phase, contractors face a perverse incentive: they may accelerate through the hazardous phase to reduce the time they are exposed to the high premium — but acceleration itself increases risk. Also, because the insurer cannot easily observe daily site conditions, it must rely on the contractor’s reporting. This creates a moral hazard: the contractor knows more about the site’s risk than the insurer does. The insurer responds with deductibles and conditions, but these blunt tools can push contractors to under-report hazards. Over time, the market may sort contractors into “safe” and “risky” based on their claims history — but a contractor with no claims may simply have been lucky, not careful. The pricing of construction risk is thus as much about *information* as about *probability*.

## A question to sit with
If a building under construction is a different risk every day, why do we insure it as a single policy with a single premium — and what would an insurance product look like that priced the risk *continuously*, updating the premium as the building rises?

2. **Contingent** — (adjective, /kənˈtɪndʒənt/) — dependent on something uncertain; possible but not certain. _Example: Insurance protects against contingent losses that may or may not occur._
3. **Insurable interest** — (noun phrase, /ˈɪnʃərəbəl ˈɪntrəst/) — a financial stake in something such that its loss would cause the policyholder harm. _Example: The owner has an insurable interest in the building because its destruction would cause financial loss._
4. **Underwrite** — (verb, /ˈʌndərˌraɪt/) — to accept financial risk in exchange for a premium, or to assess and price that risk. _Example: The insurer must underwrite the risk of a half-built tower, which changes daily._
5. **Mitigate** — (verb, /ˈmɪtɪɡeɪt/) — to make less severe or serious. _Example: The insurer may mitigate its own risk by purchasing reinsurance._
6. **Reinsurance** — (noun, /ˌriːɪnˈʃʊərəns/) — insurance taken out by an insurer to cover part of its own risk. _Example: For a massive project, the primary insurer relies on reinsurance to share the burden._
7. **Deductible** — (noun, /ˈdɪdʌktɪbəl/) — the amount the policyholder must pay out of pocket before the insurer pays a claim. _Example: A high deductible forces the contractor to bear the first layer of loss._
8. **Moral hazard** — (noun phrase, /ˈmɒrəl ˈhæzərd/) — the tendency of a party to take more risk because they are protected from the consequences. _Example: A contractor may cut corners on safety because the insurance policy shields them from full financial loss._
9. **Peak risk** — (noun phrase, /piːk rɪsk/) — the moment or phase when the probability or magnitude of loss is highest. _Example: The framing stage is the peak risk for fire because the structure is open and unprotected._
10. **Moving target** — (noun phrase, /ˈmuːvɪŋ ˈtɑːɡɪt/) — a problem or risk that changes constantly, making it hard to assess. _Example: Underwriting a construction site is pricing a moving target._
11. **Offload** — (verb, /ˌɒfˈloʊd/) — to transfer a burden or risk to someone else. _Example: The insurer offloads excess risk to a reinsurer._
12. **Claims adjuster** — (noun phrase, /kleɪmz əˈdʒʌstər/) — a person who investigates and settles insurance claims. _Example: After the fire, the claims adjuster assessed the damage to the half-built structure._
