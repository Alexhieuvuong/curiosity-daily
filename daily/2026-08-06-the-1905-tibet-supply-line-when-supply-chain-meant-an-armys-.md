# The 1905 Tibet Supply Line: When “Supply Chain” Meant an Army’s Lifeline

> Before it was a corporate buzzword, “supply chain” was a military problem — keeping an army alive across the Himalayas.

## Why this is interesting
The phrase “supply chain” may have been first published in 1905, not in a business textbook, but in a brief mention of keeping a supply line “unbroken” during the British expedition to Tibet [1]. That origin story reveals something crucial: the concept of supply chains was born from life-or-death logistics, long before it became about optimizing warehouse costs. Understanding that military root helps explain why the field still obsesses over resilience, risk, and the cost of failure.

## First principles
A supply chain is a complex logistics system of facilities that convert raw materials into finished products and distribute them to end consumers [1]. Logistics — the part of SCM that handles the efficient forward and reverse flow of goods, services, and information — is what holds the supply chain together [2]. The irreducible mechanic is simple: move the right thing, to the right place, at the right time, with minimal waste. In military logistics, the “end consumer” is a soldier, and the “product” is food, armaments, ammunition, and spare parts [2]. The 1905 British expedition to Tibet faced the ultimate version of this problem: keeping a supply chain with India unbroken [1]. The core tension — between the need for supply and the cost of supplying — is a central concern in logistics, which accounts for a significant amount of an organization's or country's operational costs [2].

## Break it into pieces
- **The “unbroken” condition**: The 1905 article noted the difficulty of keeping a supply chain with India unbroken during the British expedition to Tibet [1].
- **Tiers and dependency**: Suppliers are organized into tiers, with first-tier suppliers feeding the client directly, second-tier feeding the first, and so on [1].
- **Forward vs. reverse flow**: Logistics manages both the forward movement of goods and the reverse flow of used products back into the chain [2].
- **The sales vs. warehouse conflict**: In any chain, the sales department wants high inventory to meet demand, while the warehouse wants low inventory to cut holding costs [3].
- **The cost of logistics**: Logistics accounts for a significant share of an organization’s or country’s operational costs [2].

## Follow the incentives
The 1905 mention of “supply chain” appeared in the context of a military expedition, where the supply line was a matter of operational necessity [1]. The soldiers at the front had every incentive to demand more supplies; the officers managing the line had every incentive to keep the chain lean. The same incentive clash appears in modern SCM: sales wants inventory, the warehouse wants low holding costs [3]. The entity that profits is the one that can keep the chain unbroken at the lowest cost.

## How it echoes elsewhere
Illustrative: The same “unbroken line” logic governs electricity grids — a single downed transmission line can black out a region, and grid operators must balance the cost of redundancy against the cost of failure. Illustrative: It also appears in human physiology — the circulatory system is a supply chain for oxygen and nutrients, where a blocked artery is a broken link with immediate, severe consequences. In both cases, the system’s resilience depends on redundancy and the ability to reroute — the very principles modern SCM research now emphasizes under “resilience” and “risk management” [3].

## A real-world case
The 1905 mention in *The Independent* is the documented anchor: the article noted the difficulty of “keeping a supply chain with India unbroken” during the British expedition to Tibet [1]. The phrase’s appearance there — in a context of imperial military logistics — shows that the concept predates its corporate usage by decades. The key fact is that the first published use of the term was about keeping a military lifeline intact.

## Second-order effects
The military origin of “supply chain” may help explain a persistent bias in the field: an over-focus on efficiency (minimizing cost) at the expense of resilience (surviving shocks) [3]. Armies historically learned that the cheapest supply line is often the most fragile one — a lesson that corporate SCM has had to relearn during disruptions. The 1905 origin also suggests that the term’s meaning has shifted: from a physical line of supply to a complex network of tiers, information flows, and reverse logistics [1][2]. That shift matters because modern SCM research now flags “people dimension,” ethical issues, and transparency as underrepresented topics [3] — concerns that arise directly from the network’s increasing complexity.

## A question to sit with
If the first “supply chain” was a military lifeline where failure meant death, why did the corporate world adopt the term only when the stakes were reduced to profit and loss — and what did that translation lose in urgency?
