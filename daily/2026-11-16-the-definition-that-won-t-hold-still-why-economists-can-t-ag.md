# The Definition That Won't Hold Still: Why Economists Can't Agree on What the Informal Economy Is

> Researchers admit they cannot even agree on what "it" is.

## Why this is interesting
The informal economy is often described in a single tidy phrase — activity "neither taxed nor monitored by any form of government" [1]. That sounds crisp until you try to draw the boundary. A 2011 review of the economics, sociology, political science, and management literature concluded bluntly that "defining the informal economy proves problematic" and that current definitions "fail to converge around a unitary construct" [5]. The thing we are trying to measure, regulate, and protect may be a family of overlapping ideas wearing one name.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: an economy is a set of exchanges, and a *formal* one is those exchanges that a state can see, tax, and license. The informal economy is everything that happens in the same space but escapes that visibility [1]. The trouble is that "escapes visibility" is defined by a *relationship to the state*, not by any property of the activity itself. So the same transaction can be informal in one jurisdiction and formal in another, and the category shifts whenever the state's reach shifts. Researchers note the informal sector overlaps heavily with the formal economy rather than sitting cleanly outside it [2], and they define it variously as "unregulated by law but governed by custom or personal ties" [5] — a definition about *governance*, not just taxation. These are not the same test.

## Break it into pieces
- **Taxation vs. regulation vs. registration:** Is an activity informal because it is untaxed, unregulated, or unlicensed? Each test draws a different border [1][2].
- **Legality vs. formality:** Criminal activity (narcotics, prostitution) and merely unregistered activity get lumped together, yet they are morally and legally distinct [1].
- **Measurement:** If informal activity is excluded from GDP, how do we even know its size — and what changes when a country decides to count it [1]?
- **Custom vs. law:** Activity "governed by custom or personal ties" [5] is rule-bound, just not by statute. Is that informal, or differently formal?
- **Who counts as a worker:** The ILO frames the issue around "decent work… for all who work, whether in formal or informal contexts" [4] — a lens that treats formality as a spectrum of protection, not a binary.

## Follow the incentives
Different actors benefit from *different definitions*, which is why no single one wins. States gain revenue and control by widening the formal net, but they also gain a larger GDP by *counting* informal activity — Italy has included informal estimates in GDP since 1987, swelling it by an estimated 18%, and in 2014 several European countries folded prostitution and narcotics into official GDP, raising it 3–7% [1]. Statisticians face pressure to make the number bigger and comparable across borders. Researchers want a construct clean enough to study [5]. Workers in the informal sector bear the risk: China's informal economy is described as fast-growing and competitive, and approximately half of urban Chinese workers belonged to the informal economy as of 2004, raising "serious concerns about the lack of protections afforded to workers" [2]. And some local Chinese governments actively *encourage* informality to alleviate unemployment [2] — so the state can be both the taxer and the enabler.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same "defined by relation to an authority" problem appears in **shadow banking**: what counts as a "bank" depends on whether it takes insured deposits, so risk migrates to whatever escapes the label. It also echoes **the gig-economy classification fight**: whether a worker is an "employee" or "contractor" is likewise a boundary drawn by legal relationship, not by the nature of the work — and the boundary determines who bears risk. Illustrative: in both cases the category is a legal artifact, and actors arrange themselves around it.

## A real-world case
China illustrates how slippery the category is in practice. Its informal economy is described as standing "outside of the recognized systems of regulations, taxation, and licenses," yet it "overlaps" with the formal economy and serves "an important societal function in contributing to employment and economic growth" [2]. Roberta Neuwirth argues China's rise as a global manufacturing center "owes, in part, to its willingness to trade in the informal economy, also known as system D trade," producing unregulated goods for Africa, Latin America, and elsewhere [2]. So the same country hosts a formal manufacturing "superpower" [3] and a vast informal export machine — and the line between them is exactly what the definitional debate cannot pin down.

## Second-order effects
If the category has no fixed boundary, then **any statistic about "the informal economy" is partly an artifact of the definition chosen** — my reasoning, following from [5]'s finding that definitions don't converge. That means cross-country comparisons (say, "informality is X% here, Y% there") may be comparing different things wearing the same label. It also means policy targeting "the informal sector" may hit a moving target: formalize one slice and activity can migrate to whatever remains unseen, much as risk migrates around regulatory labels. And counting informal activity in GDP [1] changes the denominator against which debt, spending, and growth are judged — quietly rewriting the numbers politicians argue over.

## A question to sit with
If "informal" is defined only by its relationship to the state, is the informal economy a *thing* to be measured — or a *mirror* that shows us the limits of what the state can see?
