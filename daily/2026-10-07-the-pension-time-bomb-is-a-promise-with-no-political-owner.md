# The Pension Time Bomb Is a Promise With No Political Owner

> A pension is a contract that outlives everyone who signed it — and that is precisely why it keeps getting postponed.

## Why this is interesting
A pension is one of the few financial promises designed to run longer than the institution making it. The "pensions crisis" is the name we give to the moment that mismatch becomes impossible to hide [2]. What makes it fascinating is that the problem is not really about money — it is about *time horizons*, and the fact that the people who must fix it are never the people who will pay for it.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: a pension is a fund or pay-as-you-go scheme into which money is paid during a working career, and from which periodic payments support someone after they stop working [3]. That is the whole idea. There are two ways to build it. A **defined benefit** plan promises fixed retirement payments and adjusts contributions to meet them; a **defined contribution** plan fixes the contributions and lets the retirement payments be whatever the fund can afford [3]. The first type pushes the risk onto the institution; the second pushes it onto the worker. Everything else — every crisis, every reform — is a fight over which of these two you are really running.

## Break it into pieces
- **The horizon problem.** The basic difficulty is that institutions must be sustained "over far longer than the political planning horizon" [2]. A pension must survive decades; a politician must survive an election.
- **The dependency ratio.** Shifting demographics cause a lower ratio of workers per retiree, driven by retirees living longer and lower birth rates — especially relative to the Post-WW2 Baby Boom [2].
- **Pay-as-you-go arithmetic.** In pay-as-you-go systems, a decreasing total fertility rate causes lifetime pension benefits to be *smaller than lifetime contributions* [2]. The promise quietly inverts.
- **The measurement question.** How big is the gap? Estimates vary enormously, and "there is significant debate regarding the magnitude and importance of the problem, as well as the solutions" [2].
- **The legal trap.** Several governments have a constitutional obligation to provide public services, but funding is strained by an increasing dependency ratio that raises the costs of elderly care [2].

## Follow the incentives
Consider who bears what. The **retiree** has already paid in and holds a promise; they bear the risk of it being trimmed. The **worker** pays into a system whose future payout depends on the next generation's size — a bet they cannot control [2]. The **politician** faces a cost today for a benefit that lands on a successor's watch, which is exactly why targets get pushed back. The UK's own fiscal history shows this reflex: the 2010 coalition planned to eliminate the deficit by 2015/16, then admitted in 2014 the structural deficit would not close until 2017/18, pushed that to 2018/19 in March 2015 and to 2019/20 in July 2015, before the target of a return to surplus was finally abandoned by Chancellor George Osborne in July 2016 [1]. That is the horizon problem in miniature — a moving goalpost, each step taken by someone hoping the music stops on the next person's turn.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same structure appears in **sovereign debt**, where a stock of liabilities is serviced over time [1]. And it echoes in **any long-horizon infrastructure promise** — a bridge, a nuclear plant, a climate target — where the builder and the beneficiary are separated by an election cycle, and the rational move is always to defer.

## A real-world case
The clearest concrete case in the sources is United States Social Security. As of January 2026, its estimated underfunding is **$29.3 trillion**, or 1.5% of GDP over the years 2026–2100 — up from $25.1 trillion in the previous year's report [2]. The source explains what that number means: it is the amount that would have to be set aside *today* so that principal and interest would cover the shortfall between tax revenues and payouts over the next 75 years [2]. Note the direction of travel — the gap rose year over year.

## Second-order effects
If the gap is closed by shifting from defined benefit to defined contribution, the risk migrates from institutions to individuals [3] — which means the crisis does not disappear, it becomes a million private crises. If it is closed by raising contributions, the cost lands on current workers, who are already a shrinking share of the population [2]. And if it is simply deferred, the arithmetic compounds: a larger gap set aside later costs more, exactly as the US figure rose year over year [2]. My reading: the "time bomb" metaphor is slightly wrong. A bomb has a detonator. This is closer to erosion — slow, distributed, and easy to ignore until the ground gives way.

## A question to sit with
If a promise must outlive the institution that made it, is the honest move to stop making such promises — or to build institutions designed to outlive their founders?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **liability** — (noun, /ˌlaɪəˈbɪləti/) — a debt or obligation owed; something you are responsible for paying. _Example: The national debt is the outstanding stock of liabilities owed by the public sector [1]._
2. **stock measure** — (noun phrase, /stɒk ˈmɛʒə/) — a quantity measured at a single point in time, as opposed to a flow over a period. _Example: National debt is a stock measure, unlike the deficit, which measures borrowing over a period [1]._
3. **deficit** — (noun, /ˈdɛfɪsɪt/) — the amount by which spending exceeds income over a period. _Example: In 2017 the national debt rose by £46 billion due to the government's budget deficit [1]._
4. **dependency ratio** — (noun phrase, /dɪˈpɛndənsi ˈreɪʃiəʊ/) — the proportion of non-working people relative to working people. _Example: An increasing dependency ratio raises the costs of elderly care [2]._
5. **pay-as-you-go** — (adjective, /ˌpeɪ əz juː ˈɡəʊ/) — a system where current contributions fund current payouts rather than being invested. _Example: In pay-as-you-go systems, lower fertility makes lifetime benefits smaller than lifetime contributions [2]._
6. **defined benefit** — (noun phrase, /dɪˈfaɪnd ˈbɛnɪfɪt/) — a pension promising fixed retirement payments, with contributions adjusted to fund them. _Example: A defined benefit plan defines the payout; the contributions flex to meet it [3]._
7. **defined contribution** — (noun phrase, /dɪˈfaɪnd ˌkɒntrɪˈbjuːʃən/) — a pension where contributions are fixed and the payout is whatever the fund affords. _Example: Under a defined contribution plan, the retirement payment is whatever can be afforded [3]._
8. **underfunding** — (noun, /ˌʌndəˈfʌndɪŋ/) — the shortfall between what a fund owes and what it has set aside. _Example: US Social Security's estimated underfunding is $29.3 trillion [2]._
9. **deferred compensation** — (noun phrase, /dɪˈfɜːd ˌkɒmpɛnˈseɪʃən/) — pay earned now but received later, often for tax reasons. _Example: Occupational pensions are a form of deferred compensation [3]._
10. **annuity** — (noun, /əˈnjuːɪti/) — a financial product paying a fixed stream of income, often for life. _Example: Individuals without employer pensions sometimes use annuities to create guaranteed income [3]._
11. **dependency** — (noun, /dɪˈpɛndənsi/) — reliance on others for support. _Example: Rising dependency among the elderly strains public funding [2]._
12. **time bomb** — (noun, /ˈtaɪm bɒm/) — a situation that will become disastrous if not addressed. _Example: The "pensions time bomb" describes the predicted difficulty of paying for pension benefits [2]._
