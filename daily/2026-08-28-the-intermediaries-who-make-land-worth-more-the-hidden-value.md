# The Intermediaries Who Make Land Worth More: The Hidden Value Chain of Land Assembly

> Land doesn't just appreciate — someone has to *assemble* it into a sellable asset, and that invisible work decides who captures the value.

## Why this is interesting
We usually think of land value as a simple function of location and demand. But on the fast-growing edges of cities like Bengaluru, India, the real story is a chain of intermediaries — brokers, fixers, local power-holders — who stitch together scattered farm plots into a single developable parcel. Their "articulation work" is what turns dirt into a commodity, and it determines who gets paid along the way [5].

## First principles
Real estate value flows from the *use* to which land is put, which depends on demand for what that land can produce [4]. But land is rarely available in the exact shape, size, or legal status a developer needs. Land assembly is the process of combining many small, individually owned parcels into one large, legally clean tract. In places with fragmented ownership and informal records, this requires local knowledge and social connections — not just capital [5]. The value created isn't in the land itself; it's in the *coordination* that makes it usable.

## Break it into pieces
- **Who owns what?** Small farmers hold scattered plots; no single owner controls enough land for a large project [5].
- **What is "articulation work"?** The invisible labor of connecting different actors — farmers, financiers, developers — across scales, using social power and local knowledge [5].
- **Why can't a developer just buy directly?** Intermediaries are instrumental in assembling agricultural land for large projects, harnessing social power and local knowledge to create inter-scalar linkages [5].
- **Who captures the value?** The paper traces value from the landowning farmer up to a major real estate company, showing different actors capture different amounts at each step [5].
- **How does this differ from formal appraisal?** Appraisal methods like comparables or income models assume a clean, transparent market [4]; assembly work operates in the messy, negotiated space before that market exists.

## Follow the incentives
Farmers want the highest price for land they may have held for generations; developers want a large, unencumbered tract quickly; intermediaries want a cut of the spread [5]. Each intermediary — from local fixers to larger brokers — leverages relationships to create and extract value as land moves from farmer to developer [5]. The paper shows how existing forms of social power and local knowledge are harnessed to enable the creation and extraction of value in Indian real estate [5]. The state, meanwhile, taxes the final value through property taxes based on appraised worth [3].

## How it echoes elsewhere
This is the same pattern as **supply chain middlemen** in any fragmented market: think of commodity traders who buy from thousands of smallholders and sell to one large processor. The middleman's profit is the price of coordination, and their power comes from information asymmetry. It also mirrors **underwriting in finance** — a specialist who packages many small, risky assets into one large, investable one, charging a fee for the transformation.

## A real-world case
In the peri-urban frontier of Bengaluru, researchers interviewed land market intermediaries who assembled agricultural land for a large apartment complex [5]. These intermediaries operated at different scales, from local brokers with deep village ties to larger operators who connected those brokers to a major real estate company. The paper documents how social power and local knowledge were harnessed to create inter-scalar linkages, enabling value creation and extraction — and shows that the varying forms and quantum of value captured by different actors shaped the entire micro-dynamics of speculative urbanism in the region [5].

## Second-order effects
The paper reflects on the micro-dynamics of speculative urbanism and agrarian urbanization, showing how the value chain stretches from the landowning farmer up to a major real estate company [5]. Intermediaries profit from the opacity of informal land markets, and their role is central to how value is created and captured in Indian real estate [5]. And because the assembled land is then appraised at its new, higher value [1], the tax base reflects that increase — but the distribution of who captured the intermediate value is not documented in the sources.

## A question to sit with
If intermediaries are essential to making fragmented land markets work, is their profit a legitimate fee for coordination — or a rent extracted from farmers who lack alternatives?
