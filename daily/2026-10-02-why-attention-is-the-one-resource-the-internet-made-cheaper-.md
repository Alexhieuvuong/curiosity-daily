# Why Attention Is the One Resource the Internet Made Cheaper to Capture and Costlier to Spend

> Digital technology drove five costs toward zero — but the cost of *your* attention never fell. It just changed hands.

## Why this is interesting
The internet is celebrated for collapsing the cost of storing, computing, and transmitting information [5]. Yet attention — the thing information must ultimately be spent on — is finite and non-replicable. That asymmetry is the engine of the attention economy: advertising-driven companies are incentivized to maximize the time and attention their users give to their product [1]. Illustrative: the same technology that made *content* cheap also made *tracking* cheap, which is one reason attention becomes measurable and tradeable.

## First principles
Start with one irreducible fact: information is now represented in bits, and digital technology has reduced the cost of storage, computation, and transmission of data [5]. Attention economics treats human attention as a scarce commodity and applies economic theory to information-management problems [1]. Put those together and you get a hard mechanic: when one input (information) becomes nearly free while the complementary input (attention) stays fixed, all the economic pressure migrates to the scarce input. Illustrative: whoever can capture, measure, and resell that scarce input holds the leverage.

## Break into pieces
- **Which costs actually fell?** The review names five — search, replication, transportation, tracking, and verification costs [5]. The brief's own emphasis is on the *tracking* and *search* reductions.
- **What is being bought and sold?** Not attention itself but *access to* attention, mediated by advertising incentives [1].
- **Is attention a commodity or a trait?** Attention economics treats it as a scarce commodity [1], but individual capacity to sustain it varies — and at the extreme that variation is a clinical matter [2].
- **Who measures the transaction?** Tracking is one of the costs that digital technology has reduced [5].
- **Does cheaper information mean more or less attention spent?** The sources don't settle this — it's the open tension.

## Follow the incentives
Advertising-driven companies are incentivized to maximize the time and attention users give to their product [1] — that is the core profit motive, stated plainly. The digital-cost reductions in search and tracking [5] make it cheaper to find users and cheaper to watch what they do. The user bears the cost in the currency of attention, which is finite. Meanwhile, the capacity to sustain attention is not uniform: ADHD represents the extreme lower end of a continuous dimensional trait of executive functioning and self-regulation [2], and impairments from deficits in self-regulation can include poor professional performance, relationship difficulties, and health risks [2].

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same "one input goes free, the scarce complement captures the value" pattern appears in environmental economics. The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity volume is notable for how well it was promoted, and it was the specific monetary values in its appendices that received by far the most media attention [4] — even though the bulk of the volume addresses methodological issues [4]. Illustrative: that is the same mechanic — the *measurable, monetizable* slice (attention metrics; ecosystem dollar values) dominates the discourse, while the harder-to-quantify substrate (sustained cognition; ecological method) gets sidelined.

## A real-world case
Kaitlin Bennett, an American gun rights activist and conservative social media personality, received media attention in 2018 for open-carrying an AR-10 rifle at Kent State University after graduating, and is the face of the YouTube channel Liberty Hangout, which promotes the Austrian school of economics [3]. Illustrative: read through the attention-economy lens [1], a person can convert a single attention-grabbing act into a durable media channel, because the platform's incentive is to maximize time and attention, not to adjudicate the merits of the act. The content is the bait; the attention is the product.

## Second-order effects
Illustrative: if tracking costs keep falling [5], the meter on attention gets finer-grained, and the competition for the scarce input intensifies — plausibly pushing content toward whatever reliably captures time, regardless of downstream effects on the user. Illustrative: because sustained attention and self-regulation sit on a continuous trait [2], heavy capture may concentrate its functional costs on those already at the lower end of that distribution. Illustrative: and because media discourse may gravitate to the most monetizable numbers [4], the *measurement* of attention may crowd out discussion of what attention is actually for.

## A question to sit with
If information is nearly free but attention is fixed, is the attention economy a market that allocates a scarce resource efficiently — or a machine that manufactures scarcity where none needed to exist?
