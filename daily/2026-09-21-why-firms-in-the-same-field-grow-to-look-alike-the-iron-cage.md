# Why Firms in the Same Field Grow to Look Alike — The Iron Cage of Monopoly

> Competition is supposed to breed variety. So why do rivals end up as near-identical copies of one another?

## Why this is interesting
Industrial organization studies how firms and markets behave across a spectrum from competition to monopoly, using real-world features like transaction costs, imperfect information, and barriers to entry [1]. But there is a stranger puzzle hiding inside that spectrum: as organizations in a shared field mature, they tend to converge on the same structures and practices — even when they are supposedly fighting each other [5]. Understanding why sameness emerges is a window into how market power actually gets built and held.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic. A market is a setting where one or a few sellers can hold "market power" — the ability to raise prices above marginal cost or exclude competitors [2]. Industrial organization exists precisely because real markets depart from the textbook model of perfect competition, through transaction costs, imperfect information, and barriers to entry [1]. Now add a second mechanic from organizational theory: once a set of organizations emerges as a field, "rational actors make their organizations increasingly similar as they try to change them" [5]. The engine of this rationalization, the authors argue, has moved from the competitive marketplace to the state and the professions [5]. So the core idea is: market structure shapes firm behavior, but the surrounding institutions — regulators, laws, professional norms — push firms toward conformity.

## Break it into pieces
- What exactly is a "field," and when does a loose set of rivals become one that exerts pressure to conform [5]?
- The sources name three isomorphic processes — coercive, mimetic, and normative — that drive sameness [5]. Which one dominates in heavily regulated industries versus lightly regulated ones?
- How does conformity interact with market power? Does looking alike make collusion easier, or does it just raise barriers to entry [1][2]?
- Where is the line between a dominant position that is legal and behavior that is "abusive" and sanctionable [2]?
- Does the state push firms toward sameness deliberately, or as a side effect of regulation [1][5]?

## Follow the incentives
Who pays, who profits, who bears the risk? Firms bear the risk of uncertainty, and the theory suggests they imitate peers when goals are ambiguous and technology is uncertain [5] — mimicking a successful rival is a cheap way to reduce that risk. Regulators and the state set the rules that make some behaviors coercively required [5], and competition law restricts monopolies over concerns about adverse effects, while a dominant position alone is often not illegal [2]. Professionals — the third engine of isomorphism — carry norms across organizations [5]. Meanwhile the government can grant monopolies to private firms (patents, copyrights, trademarks) to incentivize risky investment, or reserve a venture for itself as a government monopoly [2][3]. Each actor is responding to its own incentive: firms to survive, regulators to control, professionals to standardize.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same convergence mechanic appears in the study of professions themselves: the very idea that "the professions" now drive rationalization [5] mirrors how standards bodies and credentialing regimes homogenize practice across an industry. Illustrative: just as two unrelated desert plants both evolve spines and thick stems, two unrelated firms under the same regulator may adopt near-identical compliance departments.

## A real-world case
The sources give us a clean institutional case rather than a single firm story. Government-granted monopolies — patents, copyrights, and trademarks — are explicitly described as examples of monopolies sanctioned by the state, often "to provide an incentive to invest in a risky venture or enrich a domestic interest group" [2]. This is a concrete instance of coercive isomorphism: the state legally forbids competition for a period, and every firm in that field must organize itself around the same legal constraint [2][5]. The result is a field where sameness is not a market outcome but a legal requirement.

## Second-order effects
If institutions, not markets, are the primary engine of rationalization [5], then the "competition" we observe may be partly theater — firms differentiating at the margin while converging on structure. One downstream consequence: barriers to entry may rise not from any single firm's dominance but from the shared norms and compliance costs that a field collectively imposes [1][5]. Another: antitrust authorities focused only on price and market share might miss homogenization as a source of durable market power [1]. And because patents and copyrights are state-granted monopolies [2], the line between "protecting innovation" and "enriching an interest group" becomes a live policy tension rather than a settled question.

## A question to sit with
If rational actors make their organizations more alike precisely as they try to change them [5], is genuine diversity in a mature industry even possible — or is convergence the default fate of any field that lasts long enough?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **isomorphism** — (noun, /ˌaɪsoʊˈmɔːrfɪzəm/) — a process by which entities become similar in form or structure. _Example: The three isomorphic processes push rival firms toward the same organizational shape [5]._
2. **coercive** — (adjective, /koʊˈɜːrsɪv/) — using force or authority to compel action. _Example: Coercive pressure from regulators can force every firm in a field to adopt identical compliance rules [5]._
3. **mimetic** — (adjective, /mɪˈmɛtɪk/) — relating to imitation; copying the behavior of others. _Example: Under uncertainty, firms engage in mimetic behavior by copying a successful rival's strategy [5]._
4. **normative** — (adjective, /ˈnɔːrmətɪv/) — establishing or relating to a standard or norm. _Example: Professional bodies exert normative pressure that standardizes how work is done across an industry [5]._
5. **rationalization** — (noun, /ˌræʃənələˈzeɪʃən/) — the process of organizing activity according to efficient, calculated principles. _Example: The engine of rationalization has shifted from the marketplace to the state and the professions [5]._
6. **oligopoly** — (noun, /ˌɒlɪˈɡɒpəli/) — a market dominated by a few sellers. _Example: Monopolies, monopsonies, and oligopolies all involve one or a few entities with market power [2]._
7. **monopsony** — (noun, /məˈnɒpsəni/) — a market with only one buyer. _Example: A monopsony lets a single buyer distort the market against its suppliers [2]._
8. **cartel** — (noun, /kɑːrˈtɛl/) — a group of providers that coordinate prices or output. _Example: A cartel differs from a monopoly because several providers act together rather than one selling alone [2]._
9. **barrier to entry** — (noun phrase, /ˈbæriər tə ˈɛntri/) — an obstacle that makes it hard for new competitors to enter a market. _Example: Resource-intensive industries can create natural barriers to entry that sustain a monopoly [1][2]._
10. **contestability** — (noun, /kənˌtɛstəˈbɪləti/) — the degree to which a market is open to potential entrants. _Example: Tirole develops the idea of contestability alongside barriers to entry and exit [4]._
11. **vertical integration** — (noun phrase, /ˈvɜːrtɪkəl ˌɪntɪˈɡreɪʃən/) — combining successive stages of production or distribution under one firm. _Example: Industrial organization studies vertical integration as one of its core issues [1]._
12. **antitrust** — (adjective/noun, /ˌæntiˈtrʌst/) — relating to laws that promote competition and restrict monopolies. _Example: Industrial organization has had significant practical impacts on antitrust law [1]._
