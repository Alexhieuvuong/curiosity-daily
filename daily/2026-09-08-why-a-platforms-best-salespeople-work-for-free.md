# Why a Platform’s Best Salespeople Work for Free

> The most powerful marketing department a platform can have isn’t on its payroll — it’s the developers who build for it, hoping you’ll show up.

## Why this is interesting
Platforms like video-game consoles and operating systems don’t just compete for users — they compete for the third-party developers who make the platform worth joining. These developers often work without direct payment from the platform owner, yet their contributions can shape which platform wins an entire market. Understanding why they participate reveals the hidden engine of modern digital monopolies.

## First principles
A network effect is the phenomenon where a product’s value to one user depends on how many other users are using it [1]. When the effect is *indirect*, two distinct groups make each other more valuable: hardware becomes more valuable to consumers as more compatible software appears, and software becomes more valuable to developers as more consumers own the hardware [1]. This creates a two-sided market — an intermediary platform that connects two groups, where each group provides network benefits to the other [2].

## Break it into pieces
- **Direct vs. indirect effects** — A telephone network gets more valuable with each new caller (direct); a game console gets more valuable with each new game developer (indirect) [1].
- **The chicken-and-egg problem** — Which side comes first: users or developers? Two-sided market theory exists largely to analyze this puzzle, as seen in standards battles like VHS vs. Beta [2].
- **Who pays and who is paid** — In many two-sided markets, one group is subsidized (free or cheap) to attract the other group, which then pays [2].
- **Platform evangelism** — Platforms deliberately recruit third-party developers, providing them resources and feedback, not as charity but to accelerate the ecosystem of complementary goods [3].
- **Quality vs. network size** — A key open question is whether network effects or platform quality matters more for competitive outcomes [4].

## Follow the incentives
The platform owner wants maximum market share, which requires a rich ecosystem of complementary goods created by independent developers [3]. Consumers prefer platforms with more software, and developers prefer platforms with more consumers [2]. This mutual dependence is why platforms often give one side free access: attracting one group is the cost of attracting the other [2].

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same logic governs credit cards: consumers want cards that more merchants accept, and merchants want to accept cards that more consumers carry [2]. The pattern also appears in recruitment platforms like Monster.com, which connects job seekers and recruiters [2]. In every case, the platform’s value comes from the cross-group externality it manages.

## A real-world case
The competition between VHS and Betamax is the classic illustration of two-sided market dynamics [2]. Two-sided markets are particularly useful for analyzing the chicken-and-egg problem of standards battles, such as the competition between VHS and Beta [2].

## Second-order effects
When platforms compete by subsidizing one side, the subsidy must be funded somehow — which is why many platforms offer one group free use in order to attract the other group [2]. Another consequence is that consumer expectations are key in determining which outcome results in markets that exhibit network effects [1].

## A question to sit with
If developers are the unpaid contributors that make platforms dominant, what happens when a platform’s governance decisions — like sudden fee changes or policy shifts — betray the very developers who built its ecosystem?

## Go deeper
- Examine what resources platform evangelists actually provide to developers — the sources note they provide resources to innovate, participate, and provide feedback [3].
- Consider the role of consumer expectations: how does a platform *signal* to developers that it will win, before it actually has?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **Network externality** — (noun, /ˈnɛtwɜːk ɪkˌstɜːˈnælɪti/) — The side effect where one user’s adoption of a product affects the value others get from it. _Example: A network externality means your choice of phone brand affects how useful my phone is._
2. **Complementary goods** — (noun phrase, /ˌkɒmplɪˈmɛntəri ɡʊdz/) — Products that enhance each other’s value when used together. _Example: Games are complementary goods to a console._
3. **Two-sided market** — (noun phrase, /ˌtuːˈsaɪdɪd ˈmɑːkɪt/) — A platform serving two interdependent customer groups. _Example: A credit card network is a two-sided market linking shoppers and stores._
4. **Bandwagon effect** — (noun, /ˈbændwæɡən ɪˈfɛkt/) — The tendency for adoption to accelerate as more people join. _Example: The bandwagon effect made the winning video format unstoppable._
5. **Positive feedback loop** — (noun phrase, /ˈpɒzɪtɪv ˈfiːdbæk luːp/) — A cycle where each step amplifies the next. _Example: More users attract more developers, which attracts more users — a positive feedback loop._
6. **Critical mass** — (noun, /ˈkrɪtɪkəl mæs/) — The threshold beyond which a network grows on its own momentum. _Example: Once a platform reaches critical mass, it no longer needs to recruit users manually._
7. **Standards battle** — (noun phrase, /ˈstændədz ˈbætl/) — A competition between incompatible technologies for market dominance. _Example: The VHS versus Betamax fight was a famous standards battle._
8. **Evangelism** — (noun, /ɪˈvændʒəlɪzəm/) — Passionate promotion of a technology to build its ecosystem. _Example: Platform evangelism turns developers into voluntary advocates._
9. **Cross-group externality** — (noun phrase, /krɒs ɡruːp ɪkˌstɜːˈnælɪti/) — The benefit one user group receives from growth in another group. _Example: Merchants benefit from a cross-group externality when more consumers join a card network._
10. **Subsidy** — (noun, /ˈsʌbsɪdi/) — A payment or price reduction given to one side to attract it. _Example: Free user access is a subsidy funded by advertisers._
11. **Equilibrium** — (noun, /ˌiːkwɪˈlɪbriəm/) — A stable outcome where no participant wants to change behavior. _Example: Multiple equilibria exist in network markets, and expectations pick the winner._
12. **Sunk investment** — (noun phrase, /sʌŋk ɪnˈvɛstmənt/) — Costs already spent that cannot be recovered. _Example: Developers who backed Betamax lost their sunk investment when VHS won._
