# The Metric That Can't Measure: Why QALYs Are Health Economics' Most Useful Unreliable Ruler

> What do you do when the number that decides who gets treated is one everyone admits is built on "unreasonable" assumptions?

## Why this is interesting
Health economics is a discipline that studies efficiency, value, and behavior in the production and consumption of healthcare [1]. But its central measurement tool — the Quality Adjusted Life Year, or QALY — is described in the source material as "very difficult to measure and relies upon assumptions that are often unreasonable" [1]. That is a remarkable admission: the field's flagship yardstick is openly contested. Understanding why a profession keeps using a tool it distrusts is a lesson in how all measurement works.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible problem. Health economics "does not follow normal rules for economics" because price and quality are "often hidden by the third-party payer system of insurance companies and employers" [1]. When the person consuming care is not the person paying for it, the usual price signal that disciplines markets goes dark [1]. So health economists need a substitute yardstick — something that lets them compare treatments, allocate scarce resources, and argue about value when money alone won't tell the story. The QALY is that substitute: one of the most commonly used measurements for treatments [1]. The mechanic is hard to execute: QALY measurement is "very difficult to measure and relies upon assumptions that are often unreasonable" [1].

## Break it into pieces
- **What is being measured?** The QALY is a commonly used measurement for treatments, but it is very difficult to measure [1].
- **What supplies the assumptions?** QALY measurement "relies upon assumptions that are often unreasonable" [1] — so its reliability is only as good as those assumptions.
- **Why is this a health-economics problem specifically?** Because the hidden-price problem (the third-party payer) removes the market's own measuring device, forcing the field to construct an artificial one [1].
- **What happens when the ruler is wrong?** Health economics is concerned with resource allocation and efficiency [4], and a measurement that is hard to make and assumption-laden [1] complicates that allocation work.

## Follow the incentives
The third-party agent — the insurer financially responsible for the care the patient consumes — is a central actor in the system [1]. The patient consumes care while price and quality are often hidden by the third-party payer system [1]. The provider is caught between. *Illustrative:* this resembles a principal-agent tangle — the payer wants comparability and control, the patient wants their own outcome, and the QALY is the compromise object they all argue over. Note also that evidence-based policymaking is described as the "foundation for effective healthcare interventions," yet financial constraints, political influences, and stakeholder conflicts "can hinder such policy implementation" [4] — meaning even a good metric collides with interests that don't want to be measured.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same pattern — a proxy metric that everyone uses while doubting it — appears in other fields. *Illustrative:* in **education**, standardized test scores stand in for "learning" and are criticized for capturing only what's easy to count. And in **environmental policy**, a single aggregate like "tonnes of CO2 equivalent" compresses wildly different harms into one comparable number. In each case, the proxy is indispensable precisely because the thing it stands for resists direct measurement.

## A real-world case
The source material frames the QALY problem directly: it is "one of the most commonly used measurements for treatments" and yet "very difficult to measure and relies upon assumptions that are often unreasonable" [1]. The case is the metric itself — a tool so central that health economists cannot do their comparative work without it, and so contested that its foundational assumptions are openly questioned [1]. *Illustrative:* imagine two treatments, one extending life by a year in full health, another extending life by two years in significant discomfort — the QALY forces a single ranking, and the ranking flips depending on the quality weights chosen.

## Second-order effects
If the field's core metric is assumption-laden, then every policy built on it inherits those assumptions — a fragility that compounds as decisions scale up. This may explain why the discipline emphasizes "a comprehensive approach that integrates health economics with policy analysis" to build "sustainability and resilience" [4]: resilience is needed precisely because the underlying numbers are contestable. A second-order risk: stakeholders who dislike a QALY-based conclusion can attack the assumptions rather than the evidence, turning measurement debates into political ones.

## A question to sit with
If a metric's assumptions are admittedly "unreasonable," is it more honest to keep using it transparently, or to abandon it for something less comparable but more defensible?
