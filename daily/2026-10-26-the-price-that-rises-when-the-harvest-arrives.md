# The Price That Rises When the Harvest Arrives

> In Punjab's grain markets, more wheat showing up at the mandi went hand in hand with *higher* prices — the opposite of what the textbook says should happen.

## Why this is interesting

The first lesson in any economics course is that supply and demand pull prices in opposite directions: more of a thing arriving should push its price down. Yet a study of major agricultural commodities in Punjab's markets found that when arrivals of wheat, paddy and maize rose, prices tended to rise *with* them [4]. That result is a genuine puzzle worth taking seriously — and untangling it teaches you why real markets are messier than the clean curves we draw.

## First principles

Start with what a commodity actually is. In economics, a commodity is a good with substantial *fungibility* — the market treats instances of it as equivalent regardless of who produced it — and its price is set by the market as a whole, not by any one seller's brand [2]. Because commodities are interchangeable, wide availability tends to shrink profit margins and makes price, rather than brand name, the thing that matters [2]. The classic mechanism for managing the risk of that price moving is the futures contract: a farmer can sell a contract for corn not yet harvested and lock in the price he'll be paid, while a cereal producer buys it to guarantee the price won't rise — each side trading away the risk it can't bear [3]. Speculators and investors buy and sell these contracts too, and in doing so they supply *liquidity* to the system [3]. So the baseline expectation is straightforward: a commodity's price is a function of its whole market [2], and a wave of new supply should, all else equal, weigh on that price.

## Break it into pieces

- **What does "correlation" actually tell us here?** The study reports a *positive and high correlation coefficient* between arrivals and prices [4] — but correlation is not causation, and something else may be moving both.
- **Which direction does the arrow point?** Do high arrivals *cause* high prices, or do expectations of high prices *draw* more grain to market?
- **Why do the four crops disagree?** Wheat and paddy arrivals grew, while maize and cotton arrivals fell — yet *all* their prices rose [4]. That split is a clue.
- **What is the "market arrival" measuring?** It is grain *reaching the market*, not necessarily grain *being produced* — a distinction that matters enormously.

## Follow the incentives

Think about who is actually choosing to bring grain to the mandi. A farmer holding a crop faces a decision: sell now, or store and sell later. If he expects prices to be *higher* later, he holds back; if he expects them to be *lower*, he rushes to sell — which is exactly when everyone else rushes too. The study's finding that prices of all four crops rose while arrivals of two of them *fell* [4] hints that arrivals and prices may be jointly driven by something upstream — expectations, timing, or the conditions of the season. Note also that the futures mechanism exists precisely to let farmers and buyers shed this timing risk [3]; where such instruments are thin or unavailable, farmers bear it directly, and their collective selling decisions can amplify price swings. (This is my reasoning from the sourced facts, not a claim the study makes.)

## How it echoes elsewhere

Illustrative: the same "more supply, higher price" puzzle can be imagined in **financial markets** — a surge of buyers into an asset might raise its price even as the "supply" of shares on offer grows, if the arrival of participants signals demand and liquidity rather than satiation. It can likewise be imagined in **labor markets** — a wave of applicants to a booming sector need not depress wages if the boom is what attracted them, with the inflow and the price as symptoms of the same underlying demand. In both imagined cases, the naive supply-demand story fails because the quantity and the price are responding to a common cause. (These are illustrative analogies, not claims drawn from the sources.)

## A real-world case

The Punjab study is the concrete case: analyzing secondary data on wheat, paddy, maize and cotton, it found positive growth in arrivals for wheat and paddy but negative growth for maize and cotton, while prices of *all* four crops showed positive and significant growth [4]. The authors themselves flag the positive, high correlation between arrivals and prices for wheat, paddy and maize as evidence that "prices increase in response to an increase in the market arrivals" [4] — a causal reading that a careful analyst would treat as a hypothesis to test, not a settled law, precisely because the direction of causation is the whole puzzle.

## Second-order effects

If arrivals and prices move together, then policies built on the assumption that "flooding the market lowers prices" may misfire — releasing buffer stocks or timing procurement around arrival peaks could interact with farmer expectations in ways the simple model doesn't predict. It also raises the stakes on **quality and trust**: where prices are volatile and information uneven, counterfeit and inferior goods can exploit the confusion, which is why researchers have proposed signal-display mechanisms, government supervision and credit-standing systems to shore up the marketing mechanism [5]. In other words, a market whose prices are hard to read is a market where cheating pays — and where institutions must substitute for the price signal's missing clarity.

## A question to sit with

If a rising tide of grain arriving at market coincides with rising prices, is the market telling us that supply is *causing* price — or that price expectations are *causing* supply? And how would you even design a study to tell the two apart?

## Go deeper

- Dig into the difference between **market arrivals** and **production**: what would change in the Punjab result if arrivals measured only the portion of harvest that farmers chose to sell?
- Explore how **futures and forward contracts** [3] would change a farmer's selling timing — and whether their absence helps explain synchronized arrivals.
- Compare the Punjab finding with the **counterfeit-goods problem** [5]: both are cases where the price signal is noisy, and both invite institutional fixes.

## Vocabulary Builder

1. **fungibility** — (noun, /ˌfʌndʒəˈbɪləti/) — the quality of being interchangeable with other identical items. _Example: The fungibility of wheat means buyers don't care which farm it came from._
2. **mandi** — (noun, /ˈmʌndi/) — a market or marketplace, especially for agricultural produce in South Asia. _Example: Farmers brought their paddy to the mandi at harvest time._
3. **correlation** — (noun, /ˌkɒrəˈleɪʃən/) — a statistical relationship in which two variables move together. _Example: The study found a high correlation between arrivals and prices._
4. **causation** — (noun, /kɔːˈzeɪʃən/) — the relationship in which one thing actually produces another. _Example: Correlation between arrivals and prices does not prove causation._
5. **liquidity** — (noun, /lɪˈkwɪdəti/) — the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold without moving its price. _Example: Speculators provide liquidity to the futures market._
6. **derivative** — (noun, /dɪˈrɪvətɪv/) — a financial instrument whose value is derived from an underlying asset. _Example: A futures contract is a derivative tied to the price of corn._
7. **speculator** — (noun, /ˈspekjuleɪtə/) — someone who buys and sells to profit from price changes rather than to use the good. _Example: Speculators trade contracts to profit from price swings._
8. **volatility** — (noun, /ˌvɒləˈtɪləti/) — the tendency of a price to fluctuate sharply. _Example: Storing grain exposes the farmer to price volatility._
9. **procurement** — (noun, /prəˈkjʊəmənt/) — the process of obtaining goods, often by a government or firm. _Example: Government procurement can shift when grain reaches the market._
10. **asymmetry** — (noun, /eɪˈsɪmətri/) — a lack of balance or equality between two sides. _Example: Information asymmetry lets sellers of inferior goods exploit buyers._
11. **inferior good** — (noun phrase, /ɪnˈfɪəriə ɡʊd/) — here, a substandard or counterfeit product passed off as genuine. _Example: Weak price signals make it easier to sell an inferior good as the real thing._
12. **amplify** — (verb, /ˈæmplɪfaɪ/) — to increase the strength or effect of something. _Example: Synchronized selling can amplify price swings._
