# The Discipline With No Canon: How Development Economics Argues About Its Own Foundations

> Unlike many other fields of economics, this one has no consensus on what students should know — and that disagreement is the field's most revealing feature.

## Why this is interesting
Development economics studies how low- and middle-income countries grow and improve their people's lives [1]. But unlike many other branches of economics, it has no consensus on what students should be taught [1]. That absence isn't a gap to be filled — it's a window into a deeper argument about whether markets, states, or something else should drive development, and how much "development" even means beyond income [3].

## First principles
Start with the irreducible question: what makes a poor country richer? Development economics deals with the economic aspects of the development process in low- and middle-income countries, focusing not only on growth and structural change but also on improving the potential of the mass of the population — through health, education, and workplace conditions, whether via public or private channels [1]. Crucially, approaches in this field may incorporate social and political factors to devise particular plans, unlike many other fields of economics [1]. And there is no consensus on what students should know, with different approaches weighing what drives convergence or non-convergence across households, regions, and countries [1]. So the "first principle" is really a fork: is development a technical growth problem, or a social-political one?

## Break it into pieces
- **Growth vs. breadth:** Is development mainly income growth, or a wider expansion of human choice? The UN's human development concept treats economic growth as "only a means of enlarging people's choices" [3].
- **State vs. market:** The "dirigiste dogma" debate — whether heavy state direction helps or harms developing economies — sits at the field's core [4].
- **Path vs. goal:** Much of the field asked how countries could transition to market-oriented frameworks; the debate was not about the goal but the path — "shock therapy" versus gradual pacing and sequencing [5].
- **Measurement:** What counts as progress — income, or living conditions, freedoms, and institutions [3]?
- **Pedagogy:** With no agreed canon, what does it even mean to train a development economist [1]?

## Follow the incentives
Who benefits from which framing? International organizations like the UN and World Bank adopted human development as a holistic way to evaluate a country's progress, considering living conditions, social relations, freedoms, and political institutions in addition to income growth [3] — a broader scorecard shifts what governments are rewarded for. Meanwhile, critics argued the "dirigiste dogma" (heavy state direction) was harmful and that its demise would benefit both economics and developing economies [4]. The incentive tension: a broad, multi-dimensional definition invites more actors (and funders) into "development," while a narrow growth definition keeps the scoreboard simple and market-friendly. Note this is my reading of the incentives, not a claim in the sources.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same "no agreed canon" pattern appears in adjacent social sciences. Economics itself is split between positive ("what is") and normative ("what ought to be") analysis, and between mainstream and heterodox schools [2] — development economics simply makes that split structural. And the human-development framing resembles how other fields broaden a single metric into a multi-dimensional index, trading precision for richness [3].

## A real-world case
The clearest documented case is the intellectual battle itself. Lal's critique of the "dirigiste dogma" — the state-directed development economics influential in the early 1980s — was extremely controversial when first published, yet he concluded its demise would benefit both economics and developing economies [4]. A later postscript warned that dirigiste tendencies had not disappeared but were "emerging in new guises" [4]. This is a case of a discipline publicly arguing over its own foundation — exactly the "no canon" condition [1].

## Second-order effects
If a field can't agree on its canon, its policy advice becomes contested by design — different schools will prescribe shock therapy or gradualism for the same country [5]. That ambiguity raises the value of context and judgment over textbook rules, and it may make the field more vulnerable to ideological capture, since there's no neutral baseline to anchor debate. It also means "development" as a scorecard can drift toward whatever dimensions are currently fashionable [3].

## A question to sit with
If a discipline genuinely cannot agree on what its students should know, is that a sign of intellectual richness — or of a field that hasn't yet earned the right to prescribe policy to anyone?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **canon** — (noun, /ˈkænən/) — a set of works or ideas accepted as authoritative in a field. _Example: Development economics famously lacks a settled canon._
2. **dirigiste** — (adjective, /ˌdɪrɪˈʒiːst/) — favoring strong central state direction of the economy. _Example: Lal attacked the dirigiste dogma of early-1980s development economics._
3. **convergence** — (noun, /kənˈvɜːrdʒəns/) — the process of poorer units catching up with richer ones. _Example: The field studies what drives convergence across countries._
4. **heterodox** — (adjective, /ˈhetərədɒks/) — not conforming to established or mainstream doctrine. _Example: Economics distinguishes mainstream from heterodox approaches._
5. **normative** — (adjective, /ˈnɔːrmətɪv/) — concerned with what ought to be, rather than what is. _Example: Normative economics advocates, while positive economics describes._
6. **holistic** — (adjective, /həʊˈlɪstɪk/) — treating a subject as a whole rather than as separate parts. _Example: Human development is a holistic way to evaluate progress._
7. **gradualist** — (adjective, /ˈɡrædʒuəlɪst/) — favoring slow, step-by-step change over abrupt reform. _Example: Some economists favored a gradualist tack over shock therapy._
8. **sequencing** — (noun, /ˈsiːkwənsɪŋ/) — the order in which reforms are carried out. _Example: The debate turned on the pacing and sequencing of market reforms._
9. **structural change** — (noun phrase, /ˈstrʌktʃərəl tʃeɪndʒ/) — shifts in how an economy's sectors and institutions are organized. _Example: Development economics targets growth and structural change._
10. **intertemporal** — (adjective, /ˌɪntərˈtempərəl/) — spanning or relating to different points in time. _Example: Intertemporal optimization is used in project analysis._
11. **dogma** — (noun, /ˈdɒɡmə/) — a principle asserted as unquestionably true. _Example: Lal branded state-directed economics a dogma._
12. **paradigm** — (noun, /ˈpærədaɪm/) — a dominant framework of assumptions and methods. _Example: The financial crisis cast doubt on the neoclassical paradigm._
