# Why New Buildings Serve the Rich First: The Economics of Housing Filtering

> Luxury towers look like a gift to the wealthy — but the mechanism underneath may quietly hand older homes to everyone else.

## Why this is interesting
A brand-new apartment building is almost always expensive, and it almost always fills with high-income tenants. That looks like the market serving the rich and ignoring the poor. But housing economists describe a process — "filtering" — in which today's luxury unit becomes tomorrow's affordable one, provided the supply keeps flowing [2]. The counterintuitive claim is that building *for* the rich can be a way of building *for* the poor. Whether that claim holds depends entirely on a single condition, and that condition is the whole story.

## First principles
Start with a durable good that ages. A housing unit commands its highest price and rent when it is brand new and depreciates as it gets older [2]. Because new units are the most expensive, they are first occupied by higher-income groups; over time they "filter" — become accessible — to successively lower-income groups [2]. This is not charity or policy; it is just the arithmetic of depreciation meeting a supply of new construction. The crucial dependency: filtering requires *sufficient supply*, whether from new construction in a growing area or from depopulation [2]. When supply is insufficient, the process can run in reverse — "upward filtering" or "reverse filtering" — where units once occupied by lower-income residents appreciate and become occupied by higher-income residents, a pattern associated with gentrification [2]. So the direction of the whole machine flips on whether enough new units are being added.

## Break it into pieces
- **The supply valve.** Filtering is downstream of supply. What counts as "sufficient," and who decides? [2]
- **The direction of the flow.** The same aging building can move *down* the income ladder (filtering) or *up* it (reverse filtering/gentrification) [2]. What determines which way it goes?
- **The vacancy chain.** When higher-income households move into new units, they "vacate old housing or stop competing for old housing" [2]. Trace who ends up in the vacated unit.
- **The spatial frame.** Urban economics studies where households and firms locate [1]; filtering is a location story as much as a price story.
- **The model's assumptions.** The monocentric city model — a disc-shaped Central Business District surrounded by residential area — was formulated in 1964 and has served as a starting point for urban economic analysis since then [1]. Does filtering behave the same in polycentric cities? [1]

## Follow the incentives
New units are sold or rented at the top of the market, where prices are highest, and are occupied by higher-income groups [2]. High-income buyers get the newest, best-located stock. Lower-income households, who cannot bid for new construction, depend on the *vacated* older stock that higher-income movers leave behind [2] — they bear the risk that if new supply stalls, they are left competing for a stock that is now appreciating instead of depreciating [2]. Local governments sit at the intersection of property taxes and development regulation [5], so their rules shape how much new supply actually gets built. Note the asymmetry: the incentive to build at the top and the lower-income household's benefit (inherit the vacated stock) point the same direction *only* when supply keeps flowing [2].

## How it echoes elsewhere
Illustrative: The same "new stock serves the top, then cascades down" pattern appears in **durable consumer goods**: new technology debuts at premium prices for early adopters, then depreciates into mass-market reach as newer generations arrive. And in **labor markets**, new entrants often take the least desirable roles while incumbents move up — the vacancy left behind is what opens opportunity. In both cases, the cascade depends on a steady flow of *new* arrivals at the top; choke the flow and the ladder stops moving.

## A real-world case
Illustrative: Imagine a growing city where a developer opens a new high-end tower. Wealthy tenants move in, vacating older mid-market apartments. Those vacated units, now a rung cheaper, draw in middle-income renters, who in turn vacate still-older units for lower-income households — a chain of moves that ends with more affordable options at the bottom. Now imagine the same city freezes new construction. No new tower means no vacated mid-market units; the older stock, now scarce, appreciates, and higher-income residents bid those units up instead — reverse filtering [2]. Same buildings, opposite outcome, decided by the supply valve. (This scenario is illustrative; the underlying mechanism — filtering under sufficient supply versus reverse filtering under insufficient supply — is drawn from [2].)

## Second-order effects
If filtering is real and supply-driven, then a policy that blocks new market-rate construction in the name of protecting affordability may, by starving the supply valve, push the system toward reverse filtering — the very gentrification it meant to prevent [2]. Conversely, a wave of new luxury construction could, over years, quietly expand the affordable stock without any subsidy, as long as the flow continues [2]. The unsettling implication is that the *intent* of housing policy (build for the poor vs. build for the rich) may matter less than the *volume* of supply. And because filtering operates through chains of moves across a metro area [2], its benefits and harms may be diffuse — hard to see, hard to credit, and therefore easy for politics to ignore.

## A question to sit with
If filtering genuinely converts today's luxury into tomorrow's affordability, why does it so rarely feel that way to the people waiting at the bottom of the chain?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **filtering** — (noun, /ˈfɪltərɪŋ/) — the process by which a housing unit becomes more affordable with age as it depreciates. _Example: Filtering turns a once-luxury apartment into an affordable one over decades._
2. **depreciate** — (verb, /dɪˈpriːʃieɪt/) — to decline in value over time. _Example: New homes depreciate as they age, which is what drives filtering._
3. **gentrification** — (noun, /ˌdʒɛntrɪfɪˈkeɪʃən/) — the process by which a neighborhood's housing appreciates and is taken over by higher-income residents. _Example: Reverse filtering is closely tied to gentrification._
4. **reverse (upward) filtering** — (noun phrase, /rɪˈvɜːrs ˈfɪltərɪŋ/) — when units once occupied by lower-income residents appreciate and become occupied by higher-income residents. _Example: In a supply-starved market, reverse filtering pushes rents up instead of down._
5. **monocentric** — (adjective, /ˌmɒnoʊˈsɛntrɪk/) — having a single center, as in a city organized around one Central Business District. _Example: The monocentric city model assumes jobs cluster in one downtown core._
6. **polycentric** — (adjective, /ˌpɒliˈsɛntrɪk/) — having multiple centers. _Example: Edge cities are a sign of polycentric urban expansion._
7. **agglomeration** — (noun, /əˌɡlɒməˈreɪʃən/) — the clustering of people or firms that yields economic benefits. _Example: Economies of agglomeration help explain why activity concentrates in cities._
8. **equilibrium** — (noun, /ˌiːkwɪˈlɪbriəm/) — a state in which supply and demand balance so that prices stabilize. _Example: Partial equilibrium analysis models supply and demand in a single market._
9. **vacancy chain** — (noun phrase, /ˈveɪkənsi tʃeɪn/) — the sequence of moves set off when a household vacates one unit and occupies another. _Example: A vacancy chain can carry affordability from the top of the market to the bottom._
10. **spatial** — (adjective, /ˈspeɪʃəl/) — relating to space or location. _Example: Urban economics focuses on spatial relationships between households and firms._
11. **externality** — (noun, /ˌɛkstərˈnælɪti/) — a cost or benefit that affects a third party not directly involved in a transaction. _Example: Development regulation often targets the externalities of new building._
12. **heterodox** — (adjective, /ˈhɛtərədɒks/) — not conforming to established or orthodox views. _Example: Institutional and Marxist economics are heterodox currents in urban analysis._
