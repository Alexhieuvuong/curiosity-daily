# Why Your Brain Sells You a Story: The Misperception of Feedback in Complex Systems

> We don't misjudge because we lack information — we misjudge because our brains are built to ignore the echoes of our own actions.

## Why this is interesting
Cognitive biases are usually studied in isolated choices — a gamble, a memory, a social judgment. But the most consequential biases may be the ones that emerge when we act inside a dynamic system, where our decisions loop back and reshape the environment we're reacting to. A 1989 experiment showed that smart, motivated people systematically tank a simple simulated supply chain — not because they're irrational, but because they literally cannot see the feedback they're creating [4].

## First principles
A cognitive bias is a systematic pattern of deviation from rationality in judgment — we build a "subjective reality" from our perceptions, and that construction, not objective input, drives our behavior [1]. Many biases are adaptive: they allow faster decisions when timeliness beats accuracy, as with heuristics [1]. But in complex, dynamic settings — where multiple actors, feedbacks, nonlinearities, and time delays interact — these shortcuts misfire catastrophically. The core mechanic is simple: when your action changes the system, and the system's change comes back to you with a delay, your brain fails to connect cause to effect. You anchor on the current state and adjust, but you ignore the fact that your own adjustment is part of what's changing the state [4]. That's the "misperception of feedback" — you treat the system as if it were static, when it's actually a loop you're inside.

## Break it into pieces
- **Anchoring and adjustment**: Subjects in the inventory experiment used a simple rule — set orders based on current stock and recent demand — but the rule ignored how their orders would affect future supply [4]. The heuristic is fine for stable environments; it's fatal in a system with delays.
- **Insensitivity to feedback**: The econometric tests showed subjects were "insensitive to the feedbacks from their decisions to the environment" — they didn't register that their own ordering behavior was causing the oscillations they were reacting to [4].
- **The gap between micro and macro**: Individual decisions, each locally reasonable, aggregate into dynamics that "systematically diverge from optimal behavior" [4]. The whole is not just different from the parts — it's worse than any part intended.
- **Cold vs. hot bias**: Biases can be cognitive ("cold," like mental noise) or motivational ("hot," like wishful thinking), and both can operate simultaneously [2]. In the experiment, the misperception is cold — a structural limitation, not a desire to fail.
- **Not just human**: Loss aversion appears in monkeys; hyperbolic discounting appears in rats, pigeons, and monkeys [2]. The misperception of feedback may be similarly deep — a feature of how nervous systems process causality, not a cultural artifact.

## Follow the incentives
In the experiment, subjects managed a simulated firm and their aggregate behavior systematically diverged from optimal performance [4]. The failure wasn't laziness or malice; it was structural. This matters beyond the lab. Behavioral economics began as a distinct field in the 1970s–80s, tracing back to Adam Smith's observations that desires shape economic behavior [3]. The field's core finding is that agents are boundedly rational — they use mental shortcuts that work locally but fail globally [3]. The incentive problem is that the misperception is invisible until it's too late. No single actor can see their contribution to the system's poor performance [4].

## How it echoes elsewhere
Illustrative: The same pattern — local rationality, global pathology — appears in financial markets, where traders respond to price movements they themselves are causing, creating bubbles and crashes. It also echoes in climate policy: nations optimize their own emissions while ignoring how their actions feed back into a global system with long delays. In both cases, the "misperception of feedback" is the operative bias: we anchor on current conditions, adjust marginally, and fail to see that our adjustments are part of what creates the crisis we're reacting to. The experiment is a microcosm of every collective action problem where the feedback is slow and diffuse.

## A real-world case
The 1989 experiment is the canonical case [4]. Subjects managed a simulated inventory distribution system with multiple actors, feedbacks, nonlinearities, and time delays. Their aggregate behavior systematically diverged from optimal. The estimated model showed they used an anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic for stock management, and the estimation identified specific "misperceptions of feedback" — most critically, insensitivity to how their own orders influenced the environment. The result wasn't noise; it was a reproducible, systematic error. The paper's title says it all: "Modeling Managerial Behavior: Misperceptions of Feedback in a Dynamic Decision Making Experiment" [4].

## Second-order effects
Illustrative: If feedback misperception is systematic, then organizations that design around it — by shortening feedback loops, making cause-effect visible, or automating decisions — may gain a structural advantage. This suggests why real-time dashboards and algorithmic trading might outperform human judgment: they don't suffer from the bias. But it also implies a darker consequence: as we delegate decisions to algorithms that optimize locally, we may embed the same misperception into code, scaling it faster than ever. The bias doesn't disappear; it just gets faster. Another second-order effect: the experiment's result raises questions about the rational-expectations assumption in economics. If agents can't perceive feedback even in a simplified simulation, how can markets be expected to clear efficiently in complex real-world systems? The answer may be that markets are efficient only when feedback is fast and simple — which is rarely the case.

## A question to sit with
If our brains are structurally incapable of perceiving feedback in complex systems, what does that mean for democracy, where citizens vote on policies whose effects unfold over decades and whose causes are diffuse?
