# The Half of China That Isn't on the Books

> A country can be the world's factory and still run half its urban workforce off the books — and that may be the point.

## Why this is interesting
China is described as the world's largest manufacturing economy and exporter, producing more than the nine next-largest manufacturers combined [3]. Yet the same economy is also described as having roughly half of its urban workers in the informal economy as of 2004 — a significant increase since the 1990s [2]. Those two facts sit awkwardly together. The tension between them is the whole story: formality and informality are not opposites here, but partners.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic. An informal economy is the part of an economy that is neither taxed nor monitored by any form of government [1]. Because it isn't monitored, its activity often isn't counted in a country's GNP or GDP [1]. That single feature — invisibility to the state's ledger — is what makes the informal sector both a problem and an opportunity. It escapes the costs of formality (taxes, licenses, regulations) but also escapes the protections those systems provide. The academic literature frames entry into informality not as a preference but as a consequence: most people enter it because of a lack of opportunities in the formal economy and the absence of other means of livelihood [4]. That is the core: informality is often a fallback, not a choice.

## Break it into pieces
- **Is informality a failure of the state or a service to it?** The sources call it "stigmatized as troublesome and unmanageable" yet also credit it with providing "critical economic opportunities for the poor" [1].
- **Where is the line between informal and criminal?** The term is sometimes used to describe marginal, unregulated, and even criminal activity, but there is "considerable overlap" between the informal and formal sectors [2].
- **Who benefits from invisibility?** If informal activity is uncounted, whose statistics and whose tax base suffer — and who quietly gains?
- **Can you formalize without destroying?** The stated policy challenge is finding a policy mix that matches the diversity of informality's drivers [4].
- **Why does the informal sector grow rather than shrink with development?** It has been expanding rapidly since the 1960s [1].

## Follow the incentives
Follow who pays, profits, and bears risk. The informal worker bears the risk: the sources flag "serious concerns about the lack of protections afforded to workers in the informal sector" [2]. The state loses tax revenue and monitoring, which is why informality is framed as a policy challenge [1]. But notice the twist: in some regions of China, local governments have adopted policies designed to *encourage* the informal economy to alleviate unemployment [2]. That reframes the incentive — a local government facing joblessness may prefer an unmonitored sector that absorbs workers over an unemployment statistic it cannot fix. Meanwhile, according to Roberta Neuwirth, China's rise as a global manufacturing center is attributed in part to its willingness to trade in the informal economy, also called System D trade [2], producing unregulated goods for Africa, Latin America, and elsewhere [2]. So the profit accrues not only domestically but to foreign buyers of cheap, unregulated goods.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same mechanic appears in **GDP accounting itself**. Italy has included estimates of informal activity in its GDP since 1987, swelling GDP by an estimated 18%, and in 2014 several European countries formally changed their GDP calculations to include prostitution and narcotics sales, prompting increases of 3–7% [1]. The pattern — activity that exists but isn't counted until someone decides to count it — is identical. A second echo is in **labor-market statistics**: the sources stress that "reliable and relevant statistics are needed" to understand informality [4].

## A real-world case
The clearest sourced case is China's System D trade. According to Roberta Neuwirth, China's rise as a global center of manufacturing owes, in part, to its willingness to trade in the informal economy, also known as System D trade [2]. This is not a marginal phenomenon: China produces unregulated goods for consumption in Africa, Latin America, and elsewhere [2]. The case shows the informal sector operating at industrial scale — not as a street-corner curiosity but as an export engine feeding global supply chains.

## Second-order effects
If a large share of output is unmonitored, then official GDP understates true activity — which means policy built on those numbers is calibrated to a phantom economy. The Italian and European examples [1] show what happens when statisticians try to correct this: measured GDP jumps without a single new good being produced. My reasoning: this creates a perverse incentive, because a government can "grow" its economy on paper simply by deciding to count what already existed. A second-order effect concerns the transition to formality: if local governments actively encourage informality to absorb unemployment [2], then formalization policies may face quiet resistance from the very officials tasked with implementing them. The informal sector, in other words, can become a political shock absorber — useful precisely because it is invisible.

## A question to sit with
If informality is often a fallback rather than a choice [4], and if some governments deliberately encourage it [2], then is the real policy goal to *formalize* the informal economy — or merely to make it visible enough to tax without destroying the livelihoods it sustains?

## Go deeper
- **The measurement problem:** How do statisticians actually estimate activity that is, by definition, unmonitored? The Italy and 2014 European examples [1] are a starting point.
- **The entrepreneurship angle:** The academic literature integrates entrepreneurship theory with institutional and collective identity theories to ask how opportunities are recognized and exploited in the informal economy [5] — a useful frame for asking whether informality is anti-entrepreneurial or a form of it.
- **The global supply chain:** Trace how unregulated goods move from Chinese producers to consumers in Africa and Latin America [2], and ask who along that chain bears the regulatory risk.

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **informal economy** — (noun phrase, /ɪnˈfɔːrməl ɪˈkɒnəmi/) — the part of an economy neither taxed nor monitored by government. _Example: Half of China's urban workers belonged to the informal economy as of 2004._

3. **stigmatize** — (verb, /ˈstɪɡmətaɪz/) — to mark something as shameful or disreputable. _Example: The informal sector is sometimes stigmatized as troublesome and unmanageable._
4. **unregulated** — (adjective, /ʌnˈreɡjʊleɪtɪd/) — not controlled by official rules or laws. _Example: China produces unregulated goods for consumption abroad._
5. **alleviate** — (verb, /əˈliːvieɪt/) — to make a problem less severe. _Example: Some local governments encourage the informal economy to alleviate unemployment._
6. **livelihood** — (noun, /ˈlaɪvlihʊd/) — the means by which a person earns a living. _Example: Many enter the informal economy in the absence of other means of livelihood._
7. **formalization** — (noun, /ˌfɔːrməlaɪˈzeɪʃən/) — the process of bringing activity under official rules and records. _Example: Reliable statistics are needed to monitor progress towards formalization._
8. **nominal GDP** — (noun phrase, /ˈnɒmɪnəl ˌdʒiːdiːˈpiː/) — economic output measured at current prices, without adjusting for inflation. _Example: China has the world's second-largest economy by nominal GDP._
9. **purchasing power parity** — (noun phrase, /ˈpɜːrtʃəsɪŋ ˈpaʊər ˈpærəti/) — a measure comparing economies by what money can actually buy. _Example: By purchasing power parity, China has been the world's largest economy since 2016._
10. **discrepancy** — (noun, /dɪˈskrepənsi/) — a difference between things that should match. _Example: The discrepancy between counted and uncounted output can distort policy._
11. **shock absorber** — (noun phrase, /ʃɒk əbˈzɔːrbər/) — something that cushions the impact of a disruption. _Example: The informal sector can act as a shock absorber for unemployment._
12. **multilevel** — (adjective, /ˌmʌltiˈlevəl/) — operating across several scales or layers at once. _Example: The study employs a multilevel perspective linking entrepreneurship and institutions._
