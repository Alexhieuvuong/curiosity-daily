# Why a Unicorn Is a Rarity That Gets Manufactured

> Aileen Lee named the unicorn after a mythical animal — then the herd grew to 1,248. What changed: the animals, or the counting?

## Why this is interesting
The word "unicorn" was chosen in 2013 to signal statistical rarity: a private company worth over $1 billion [2]. Yet by May 2024, CB Insights counted 1,248 of them worldwide [2]. Either the world suddenly got far more extraordinary, or the machinery that produces and prices these companies changed. The answer matters because unicorn valuations quietly set the expectations — and the risk — for an entire financing ecosystem.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: venture capital is private equity financing given to startup, early-stage, and emerging companies deemed to have high growth potential, in exchange for equity — an ownership stake [1]. Because startups face high uncertainty, these investments have high rates of failure [1]. So the core trade is stark: the investor accepts a high chance of losing everything in return for "significant control over company decisions, in addition to a significant portion of the companies' ownership" [1]. The payoff only arrives at an "exit" — an IPO, a merger, a sale to a financial buyer, or a sale to a trading company like a competitor [1]. A unicorn is simply the label for a private company whose valuation has crossed $1 billion [2]. The valuation is a marker, not a bank balance.

## Break it into pieces
- **Rarity vs. counting:** If the term was coined in 2013 to represent statistical rarity [2], what does a herd of 1,248 by 2024 tell us — about startups, or about how valuations are assigned?
- **The exit clock:** VC returns depend on an eventual exit event [1]. What happens when exits slow but valuations keep rising?
- **Control as currency:** VCs extract significant control alongside ownership [1]. Who actually steers a unicorn?
- **The tiering problem:** Decacorn, centicorn, hectocorn, terracorn [2] — do these labels inform, or do they inflate?
- **The seed-to-Series-A gap:** Seed funding validates a concept or builds a prototype [1]; institutional growth money arrives at Series A [1]. Where do most companies die?

## Follow the incentives
The venture capitalist bears the risk of financing startups, hoping some become successful [1] — and is compensated with control plus a large ownership slice [1]. The founder trades autonomy for capital that is otherwise hard to get: VC is attractive precisely for companies too small for public markets and too unproven for a bank loan or debt offering [1]. The exit buyer — public markets, an acquirer, or a secondary-market financial buyer [1] — pays the price that crystallizes the paper valuation. Note the asymmetry: the valuation is set privately, while the failure risk is borne by the fund's investors. When interest rates rose and borrowing got costlier, many unicorns saw valuations fall in 2022 [2] — a reminder that the label is sensitive to the cost of money, not just to company performance.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same pattern appears in **real estate appraisal**: a property's "value" is an opinion produced by comparable sales, not a settled transaction — until someone actually buys. And in **academic prestige**: a university's ranking is a manufactured signal that attracts resources, which then makes the signal self-fulfilling. In both, the label does work before any cash changes hands.

## A real-world case
The first modern venture capital firm, American Research & Development Corporation (ARDC), was founded in 1946 [2]. Its breakout investment — $70,000 in Digital Equipment Corporation in 1957 — became the defining success of early venture capital and is considered the first "unicorn"-level return investment, with ARDC's Dorothy Rowe as lead board member [2]. This is instructive: the outsized return came from a single concentrated bet, decades before the word "unicorn" existed. The rarity was real; the vocabulary came later.

## Second-order effects
If the unicorn label is a manufactured signal, then its proliferation changes behavior downstream: founders may optimize for the valuation milestone rather than durable cash flow. Investors, meanwhile, may lean harder on control provisions [1] to protect themselves against valuations they privately doubt. And when the cost of borrowing rises, the correction shows up as fallen valuations [2] rather than failed companies. That lag is where the real risk hides.

## A question to sit with
If a valuation is a private opinion that only becomes real at an exit [1], who should bear the cost when the opinion turns out to be wrong — the founder, the fund, or the investor that backed the fund?
