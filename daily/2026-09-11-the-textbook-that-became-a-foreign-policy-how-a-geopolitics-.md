# The Textbook That Became a Foreign Policy: How a Geopolitics Manual Shaped a State

> What happens when a book stops being read and starts being obeyed?

## Why this is interesting
Geopolitics is usually described as a *method* — a way of studying how geography shapes politics, not a set of marching orders [1]. Yet one geopolitics book, published in 1997, was reportedly well received in Russia, used as a textbook in the Academy of the General Staff, and taken seriously by military and foreign-policy elites [3]. That raises a genuinely strange question: can a theory of geography become an instrument of statecraft, rather than just a description of it?

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic. Geopolitics studies the effects of Earth's geography on politics and international relations, focusing on political power linked to geographic space — territorial waters, land, and the wealth of natural resources [1]. It is a *method* of studying foreign policy to understand, explain, and predict international political behavior through geographical variables like climate, topography, demography, and natural resources [1]. Note the word *predict*: a good geopolitical theory claims to tell you what states will do given their position on the map. The moment a theory claims predictive power, it invites a second move — using it prescriptively, as "an aid to statecraft" [1]. That is the pivot: description quietly becomes instruction.

## Break it into pieces
- **Theory vs. doctrine:** When does an analytical framework stop explaining behavior and start prescribing it? [1]
- **Who legitimizes a text?** A book's influence depends less on its arguments than on who adopts it — here, military academies and elites [3].
- **Contested authorship:** Dugin credits General Nikolai Klokotov as co-author and inspiration, though Klokotov denies this [3]. Why does credit matter so much in doctrinal texts?
- **The academic critique:** Reviewers of the geopolitics-of-energy literature note a recurring failure to even define "geopolitics," plus limited use of forecasting and a lack of theorization [4]. If the field is this loose, what stops loose ideas from hardening into policy?

## Follow the incentives
Consider who benefits from a geopolitics *textbook* becoming doctrine. An institution like a general staff gains a ready-made, teachable framework that converts geography into strategy — useful for training and for justifying decisions [3]. A political analyst gains standing when powerful figures take an interest in his work [3]. Meanwhile, the academic reviewers bear a different burden: they must police rigor, and they flag that much of the field lacks theorization and clear definitions [4]. The asymmetry is telling — the people rewarded for *clarity of doctrine* are not the same people rewarded for *honesty about uncertainty*.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same pattern appears in energy. The geopolitics of renewable energy has roots in the 1970s and 1980s but most of its literature appeared only from 2010 onward [4] — a young, fast-growing field where confident claims can outrun evidence. And the energy transition is explicitly framed as a *twofold* process: high-carbon transitions (phasing out fossil fuels) and low-carbon transitions (deploying new technologies) [5]. Just as a geopolitics text can harden into doctrine, a transition narrative can harden into policy before the theory is settled.

## A real-world case
The clearest sourced case is the book itself: *The Foundations of Geopolitics: The Geopolitical Future of Russia*, by Aleksandr Dugin, published in 1997 [3]. According to the source, it was well received in Russia, has had significant influence within the Russian military, police forces, and foreign policy elites, and has been used as a textbook in the Academy of the General Staff [3]. Colonel General Leonid Ivashov, head of the International Department of the Russian Ministry of Defence, helped draft it [3]. (Note: the source reports these claims; it does not independently verify the book's causal impact on policy.)

## Second-order effects
If a contested text can be taught as doctrine, several downstream effects follow — my reasoning, not established fact. First, it becomes hard to distinguish *prediction* from *self-fulfillment*: actors trained on a framework may behave in ways the framework predicts, not because geography compelled them but because they were taught to expect it [1][3]. Second, the field's own weaknesses — undefined terms, thin theorization — make it easier for any single text to fill the vacuum [4]. Third, as energy systems transform, the same dynamic could recur: a confident narrative about who "wins" and "loses" the transition could harden into policy before the evidence matures [4][5].

## A question to sit with
If a theory of geography can be taught as a doctrine, how would we ever tell the difference between a state following the map and a state following the textbook?

## Vocabulary Builder

2. **statecraft** — (noun, /ˈsteɪtkræft/) — the skillful management of state affairs, especially foreign policy. _Example: Some scholars argue geopolitics should serve as an aid to statecraft._
3. **doctrine** — (noun, /ˈdɒktrɪn/) — a set of principles taught or officially held by an institution. _Example: A textbook can harden into doctrine once elites adopt it._
4. **prescriptive** — (adjective, /prɪˈskrɪptɪv/) — telling people what should be done, rather than merely describing. _Example: A predictive theory can quietly become prescriptive._
5. **theorization** — (noun, /ˌθɪəraɪˈzeɪʃən/) — the act of building systematic explanations. _Example: Reviewers criticized the field for a lack of theorization._
6. **legitimize** — (verb, /lɪˈdʒɪtɪmaɪz/) — to make something seem acceptable or authoritative. _Example: Adoption by a military academy can legitimize a contested text._
7. **self-fulfilling** — (adjective, /ˌself fʊlˈfɪlɪŋ/) — coming true because it was expected to. _Example: A taught framework may become a self-fulfilling prophecy._

9. **asymmetry** — (noun, /eɪˈsɪmətri/) — a lack of balance between two sides. _Example: There is an asymmetry between those rewarded for clarity and those rewarded for honesty._
10. **deconstruct** — (verb, /ˌdiːkənˈstrʌkt/) — to analyze something to reveal its hidden assumptions. _Example: Critical geopolitics deconstructs classical theories._
11. **forecasting** — (noun, /ˈfɔːrkæstɪŋ/) — predicting future conditions from present data. _Example: The literature makes limited use of forecasting methodologies._
12. **transition** — (noun, /trænˈzɪʃən/) — a process of change from one state to another. _Example: The energy transition is framed as a twofold process._
