# The Textbook That Rewrote a Superpower's Energy Map

> A 1997 book was used as a textbook at Russia's military academy — and decades later, energy markets moved in ways its author would recognize.

## Why this is interesting
Most of us assume geopolitics is shaped by treaties, elections, and armies. But one strand of geopolitical thinking — codified in a single book by Aleksandr Dugin — has had significant influence within Russia's military, police, and foreign-policy elites and has been used as a textbook at the Academy of the General Staff [3]. Meanwhile, the actual energy map has been shifting: sanctions on Russia redirected European markets toward US and Qatari liquefied gas, and China is now leading the renewable-energy transition with massive solar and wind investment [4]. The interesting question is how doctrine and market reality interact — do ideas steer resources, or do resources quietly rewrite the ideas?

## First principles
Geopolitics is, at its core, the study of how Earth's geography — climate, topography, demography, natural resources — shapes politics and international relations [1]. It focuses on political power tied to geographic space: territorial waters, land, and the wealth of natural resources [1]. Energy is among the most consequential of those resources: as one analysis puts it, if you had to pick a single non-war, non-pandemic factor on which civilization's fate depends, it would be energy — social stability and industrial growth are tied to having enough of it [5]. So the irreducible mechanic is simple: whoever controls or supplies energy controls a lever over other states' stability.

## Break it into pieces
- Does a geopolitical *doctrine* actually drive state behavior, or merely describe and justify it after the fact?
- How much of energy "control" is about owning resources versus controlling the *routes and markets* they flow through?
- Can a country be an energy superpower in fossil fuels and a laggard in renewables at the same time — and what does that do to its leverage?
- When sanctions reroute supply, who gains the new position of power, and is that gain permanent or provisional?
- Is the "energy transition" a shift in *who* holds leverage, or just a shift in *what* the leverage is made of?

## Follow the incentives
Energy producers profit from scarcity and from being hard to replace; that gives them an incentive to keep buyers dependent. The 2023 study found the US became the largest oil and gas producer, while sanctions on Russia pushed European markets toward US and Qatari liquefied gas — reshaping the balance of power [4]. So the *sanctioning* coalition and the *alternative* suppliers both gain leverage, while the sanctioned producer loses its captive market. On the renewables side, China leads the transition through huge solar and wind investment [4], which buys it a different kind of influence: not control of fuel, but control of the manufacturing and technology supply chain. Meanwhile, deployment of renewables still faces obstacles — fossil-fuel subsidies, lobbying by incumbent power providers, and local opposition to land use [2] — meaning incumbents bear little risk from delay, while the public bears the climate risk.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same mechanic — a doctrine or framework quietly shaping decisions long before its effects are visible — appears in **monetary policy**, where a central bank's intellectual framework shapes choices that markets only feel later. It also echoes **trade policy**: just as energy sanctions reroute supply chains, tariffs reroute who trades with whom, and the "winners" are often the substitute suppliers nobody planned for. Illustrative: in both cases, the *stated* goal (pressure, protection) and the *revealed* effect (a new map of dependence) diverge.

## A real-world case
The clearest sourced case is the post-sanction reshuffling of European gas markets. The 2023 study reports that sanctions on Russia shifted European markets toward US and Qatari liquefied gas, reshaping the balance of power in the energy sector [4]. This is a concrete instance of the core mechanic: a political decision (sanctions) did not simply "punish" a producer — it *re-plumbed* who supplies whom, handing new leverage to alternative exporters. The doctrine-and-market tension is visible here: the outcome was driven less by any single geopolitical textbook than by the hard arithmetic of who could deliver gas.

## Second-order effects
If leverage migrates from *owning fuel* to *controlling supply chains and routes*, then the countries that win the next era may be manufacturers and shippers, not oil states — my inference from the study's finding that China leads via investment rather than resource ownership [4]. A second effect: if renewables keep growing (34% of global electricity in 2025, projected over 43% by 2030 [2]), fossil-fuel producers face a shrinking captive market, which may make them more aggressive about locking in remaining buyers — an incentive-driven prediction, not a sourced fact. A third: sanctions that reroute supply can create *new* dependencies (e.g., on a single alternative supplier), trading one vulnerability for another.

## A question to sit with
If a geopolitical doctrine is influential enough to be taught to a military elite [3], but the actual energy map is redrawn by sanctions and investment arithmetic [4], which one is really steering — the idea, or the incentive the idea was built to rationalize?
