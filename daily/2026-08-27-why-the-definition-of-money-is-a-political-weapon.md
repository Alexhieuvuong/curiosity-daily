# Why the Definition of Money Is a Political Weapon

> The same object can be "money" in one era and a trinket in another — and the fight over its definition decides who gets to count as rich.

## Why this is interesting
The history of money is not just a story of coins and banknotes — it is a story of *definitions* being stretched, narrowed, and weaponized. A 1959 study of African subsistence economies found that economists assigned three or four qualities to money when discussing their own society or medieval and modern societies, yet when examining ancient history or societies studied by anthropologists, they allowed only one defining characteristic to dominate [5]. That inconsistency reveals something uncomfortable: what we call money often depends on who is doing the defining.

## First principles
Money is any item or verifiable record generally accepted as payment for goods, services, and debts [2]. Its core functions are: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value, and sometimes standard of deferred payment [2]. Historically, money emerged as a commodity with intrinsic value, but nearly all modern systems use fiat money, which has no use value and is backed only by social convention and government declaration as legal tender [2]. The key mechanic is *acceptance*: money works because people agree it works, not because it is inherently valuable.

## Break it into pieces
- **Commodity vs. fiat vs. representative money** — Commodity money has intrinsic value; representative money is exchangeable for something with intrinsic value; fiat money has only nominal value [1][2].
- **The "definition trick"** — Economists in the 1950s gave money three or four functions when discussing modern societies, but allowed only one dominant characteristic when studying ancient or anthropologically-studied societies [5]. This inconsistency let them declare that money "didn't exist" in places where it clearly functioned.
- **Legal tender as a coercive act** — Fiat money's value comes from government declaration that it must be accepted for "all debts, public and private" [2]. That is a legal command, not a market outcome.
- **Bank money vs. physical money** — Most money in developed countries is not cash at all but bank balances — entries on ledgers [2]. The "real" money is invisible.
- **The unit of account problem** — If money must be a store of value, then hyperinflationary currencies fail; if it must be a medium of exchange, then cattle or cowrie shells might qualify. Which function you prioritize changes the answer.

## Follow the incentives
Who benefits from defining money narrowly? The 1959 study suggests that claims of pre-colonial African money "tend to evaporate or to emerge as tricks of definition" — meaning the definition itself determines what counts as money [5]. Who benefits from defining money broadly? The same study criticizes the inconsistency of applying different standards to different societies [5]. Governments benefit from fiat money because it lets them declare what must be accepted as payment [2]. Banks benefit from bank money because it forms by far the largest part of broad money in developed countries [2]. The risk-bearer is the ordinary holder of fiat money, whose savings can be inflated away by the very authority that declares the money legal.

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same "definition as weapon" pattern appears in **intellectual property law**: what counts as a "patentable invention" shifts with commercial interests, and the definition decides who profits. It also appears in **linguistics**: whether a regional dialect is called a "language" or a "dialect" is often a political decision, not a linguistic one. In both cases, the boundary-drawing is where the power lies. (Illustrative: These parallels are offered as analogies, not drawn from the cited sources.)

## A real-world case
The 1959 paper on African subsistence economies documents this exact phenomenon [5]. The author notes that claims of pre-colonial African money "tend to evaporate or to emerge as tricks of definition" — meaning that whether an object counted as money depended on which single function the researcher chose to emphasize [5]. The asymmetry was not about the objects — it was about the assumptions baked into the definition.

## Second-order effects
If money is defined by function rather than form, then the entire history of economic development needs rewriting: African societies with sophisticated exchange systems were not "moneyless" — they were just using different instruments. This reframing challenges the narrative that colonization brought economic progress. Conversely, if fiat money is defined purely by legal declaration, then its value rests on state power — which means a government that loses legitimacy (through hyperinflation or collapse) destroys the savings of everyone who trusted it. The definitional choice has real distributional consequences: narrow definitions erase non-Western economic history; broad definitions can legitimize state coercion.

## A question to sit with
If a cryptocurrency is accepted by millions of people for payment but is not legal tender in any country, is it money — or is it just a commodity with a marketing campaign?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **Fiat** — (adjective, /ˈfiːæt/) — Declared by authority rather than backed by intrinsic value. _Example: Modern dollars are fiat money, valuable only because the government declares them legal tender._
2. **Intrinsic** — (adjective, /ɪnˈtrɪnsɪk/) — Belonging naturally; inherent. _Example: Gold has intrinsic value because it can be used in jewelry and electronics, unlike paper notes._
3. **Legal tender** — (noun phrase, /ˈliːɡəl ˈtɛndər/) — Money that must be accepted for payment of debts by law. _Example: The U.S. dollar is legal tender for all debts, public and private._
4. **Emergent** — (adjective, /ɪˈmɜːrdʒənt/) — Arising spontaneously from interactions rather than design. _Example: Money was historically an emergent market phenomenon, not a government invention._
5. **Deferred payment** — (noun phrase, /dɪˈfɜːrd ˈpeɪmənt/) — A payment obligation postponed to a future date. _Example: A loan contract uses money as a standard of deferred payment._
6. **Subsistence economy** — (noun phrase, /səbˈsɪstəns ɪˈkɒnəmi/) — An economy where production meets basic needs with little surplus. _Example: The 1959 study examined how money impacted an African subsistence economy._
7. **Evaporate** — (verb, /ɪˈvæpəreɪt/) — To disappear or dissolve, often under scrutiny. _Example: Claims of pre-colonial African money tended to evaporate when examined closely._
8. **Asymmetry** — (noun, /eɪˈsɪmətri/) — Lack of equality or correspondence between parts. _Example: The asymmetry in how economists defined money for different societies revealed bias._
9. **Coercive** — (adjective, /koʊˈɜːrsɪv/) — Using force or threats to compel action. _Example: Legal tender laws are coercive because they compel acceptance of state money._
10. **Nominal** — (adjective, /ˈnɒmɪnəl/) — Existing in name only; not corresponding to real value. _Example: Fiat money has only nominal value, not use value._
11. **Redistribution** — (noun, /ˌriːdɪstrɪˈbjuːʃən/) — The reallocation of wealth or resources. _Example: The definition of money has redistributional consequences, deciding who counts as economically legitimate._
12. **Legitimacy** — (noun, /lɪˈdʒɪtɪməsi/) — The quality of being accepted as valid or lawful. _Example: A government that hyperinflates its currency loses legitimacy and destroys trust in its money._
