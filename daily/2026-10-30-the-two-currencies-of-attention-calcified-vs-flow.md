# The Two Currencies of Attention: Calcified vs. Flow

> If attention is money, why does some of it sit in a vault while the rest circulates — and who decides which is which?

## Why this is interesting
We usually talk about attention as a single quantity: minutes, clicks, eyeballs. But recent work argues we should split it into two distinct kinds — one that accumulates and one that flows — and that this distinction may explain why capturing attention has become "paramount" for products, political messages, and even research [5]. Once you see attention as a *currency* rather than a *resource*, the whole question shifts: not "how much do you have?" but "what kind, and what can it buy?" [5].

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic. Attention economics treats human attention as a **scarce commodity** and applies economic theory to managing information problems [1]. Advertising-driven companies, in particular, have incentives to **maximize the time and attention** users give their product [1]. The 2024 paper pushes further: it argues attention is becoming "the defining currency that moves individuals, exchanges, and many other elements of society," and proposes a **dual-stream model** distinguishing *calcified* attention from *flow* attention [5]. The word "currency" is doing real work here — a currency is something you can accumulate *and* exchange, and the paper explicitly discusses attention being "accumulated and exchanged like a currency" [5].

## Break it into pieces
- What exactly separates **calcified** from **flow** attention — is it duration, convertibility, or something about whether it can be *spent*? [5]
- The older 1997 paper frames value as a **process** — "the process by which potential value can be translated in an economy" — and argues the valuing process depends on the *nature of the medium* through which attention is given and sought [4]. Does the medium determine which stream you're in?
- If attention is a currency, what is the "exchange rate," and who sets it? [5]
- The 1997 paper ties sustainable growth to the **integration between attention and action**, plus a related **literacy** [4]. Is flow attention the kind that converts into action, while calcified attention just sits?
- Is "calcified" a compliment or a warning — accumulated stock, or frozen and unusable?

## Follow the incentives
Advertising-driven companies have a structural incentive to maximize time and attention [1]. That incentive doesn't care *which* stream it captures — it just wants more. But the two streams may serve different masters: a product, a political message, or a research finding all succeed by "capturing and holding" attention [5], and "holding" implies duration, not just a glance. The 1997 paper adds a caution: sustainable growth of an attention economy depends on attention being *integrated with action* and on building *literacy* [4]. My reading: platforms are paid in flow (constant circulation), while users may be left holding calcified stock they never learn to spend.

## How it echoes elsewhere
Consider **financial currencies**: a currency's usefulness depends on liquidity — how easily it converts into goods — and a "calcified" holding is like money under a mattress: stored but inert. Illustrative: the dual-stream idea maps onto the liquidity/illiquidity distinction. A second echo is **human capital**: the same tension — accumulated versus circulating — recurs in economic thinking about stocks and flows. The 2024 paper itself connects the attention economy to "the institutional foundations of modernity" [5], suggesting this isn't a niche metaphor but a structural parallel.

## A real-world case
The clearest grounded case is the **1997 debate** the paper builds on: it responds to the "attention economy" debate "initiated by Michael Goldhaber and Rishab Aiyer Ghosh," and introduces the notion of a *valuing process* to explain how potential value gets translated in an economy [4]. The paper's central claim is that the valuing processes applicable to attention **depend on the nature of the media** through which attention is given and looked for [4]. That is a concrete instance of the core mechanic: the medium shapes whether attention can be converted into value at all.

## Second-order effects
If attention truly becomes a defining currency [5], then *literacy* — the ability to convert attention into action [4] — becomes a form of financial literacy, and the attention-poor may be those who hold currency they cannot spend. My analysis: a dual-stream model implies that maximizing raw time-on-platform [1] could inflate the *flow* stream while degrading the *calcified* stock. Illustrative: much as printing money can erode a currency's value, that would mean the very incentive that drives the attention economy [1] might, over time, undermine the currency it depends on — a tension the 1997 paper's emphasis on *sustainable* growth anticipates [4].

## A question to sit with
If attention is a currency with two streams, is the goal to *accumulate* calcified attention or to keep flow attention *circulating* — and can a society be rich in one while bankrupt in the other?

## Go deeper
- Read the 1997 paper's "valuing process" argument [4] alongside the 2024 dual-stream model [5] and ask where they agree and where they diverge.
- Trace how the *medium* (text, feed, video) might change which stream attention falls into [4].
- Explore what "attention literacy" [4] would actually look like as a teachable skill — and who would pay to teach it.
