# When the Oil Weapon Goes Green: How the Energy Transition Rewrites Geopolitical Power

> The same geography that made some nations energy superpowers may soon make others the new powerbrokers — and the old ones the biggest losers.

## Why this is interesting
For over a century, geopolitical power has flowed from who controls fossil fuel reserves. But the energy transition is quietly redrawing that map: former hydrocarbon exporters will likely be the greatest losers from the energy transition [4]. The question isn't just "who has the oil?" anymore — it's "who has the minerals, the technology, and the grid?"

## First principles
Geopolitics is the study of how Earth's geography — climate, topography, demography, and natural resources — shapes political power and international relations [1]. For most of the 20th century, that meant one thing: controlling fossil fuel reserves. An "energy superpower" is a country that supplies large amounts of energy resources to many others, giving it the potential to influence world markets for political or economic gain — by influencing prices or withholding supplies [3]. Renewable energy changes the geography of power because the sun and wind are everywhere, but the critical materials needed to capture them are not [2][4]. The energy transition is therefore not the end of energy geopolitics — it's a re-mapping of it.

## Break it into pieces
- **Who wins, who loses?** Former hydrocarbon exporters are likely to be the greatest losers from the energy transition [4].
- **What's the new "oil"?** Critical minerals and cybersecurity become the new flashpoints, potentially exacerbating security risks and geopolitical tensions [4].
- **Transition vs. end-state:** The geopolitics of the *transitional phase* (where we are now) differs fundamentally from the geopolitics of a *post-transition world* — yet most analyses fail to distinguish between them [4].
- **The coal blind spot:** Much of the literature focuses on oil producers, but countries heavily reliant on coal — like Australia and China — are often overlooked [4].
- **Abundance, not scarcity:** We've shifted from an era of fossil fuel scarcity to one of fossil fuel abundance, where the challenge is constraining production, not securing supply [5].

## Follow the incentives
Fossil fuel exporters have incentives to delay the transition: their wealth and global influence depend on continued demand [4]. Renewable energy is now cheaper and cleaner [2]. Meanwhile, the International Energy Agency estimates that 90% of global electricity must come from renewables by 2050 to reach net zero — but current expansion is far from that pace, even in wealthy G7 and EU economies [2]. The tension is structural: those who profit from the old system resist the new one. Fossil fuel subsidies and lobbying by incumbent providers remain major obstacles [2].

## How it echoes elsewhere
The same pattern appears in **technological disruption in industry**: incumbent firms with sunk costs in old technologies resist new ones even when the new technology is superior. Illustrative: think of how the internal combustion engine outlasted early electric vehicles, or how legacy media fought the internet. It also mirrors **resource curse dynamics in development economics**: countries rich in a single resource often fail to diversify, and when the resource's value collapses, their entire political-economic system is exposed. The energy transition is, in effect, a slow-motion version of both patterns playing out at the scale of the global order.

## A real-world case
Russia is widely recognized as an energy superpower, and the United States is currently the world's leading producer of total energy, petroleum, and liquefied natural gas (LNG), as well as the leading exporter of LNG [3]. As of 2024, these two countries exemplify the old geopolitics: their global influence is tied to fossil fuel exports. But the academic literature warns that former hydrocarbon exporters will likely be the greatest losers from the energy transition [4]. Illustrative: imagine a country whose entire national budget, military, and diplomatic leverage rest on exporting oil — when demand peaks and declines, its geopolitical weight evaporates with it, and it has no renewable infrastructure or technology to replace that influence.

## Second-order effects
If former hydrocarbon exporters lose power, the global balance shifts — but this creates new dependencies. The same literature that celebrates renewable energy's advantages for international security also warns that critical materials and cybersecurity could *exacerbate* tensions [4]. There's also a subtler risk: the academic literature itself has blind spots, over-focusing on oil producers while neglecting coal-dependent nations [4]. If analysts misread the map, policymakers will too — and the transition could create instability in places no one was watching.

## A question to sit with
If the energy transition is inevitable, what happens to the countries that built their entire political systems — their constitutions, their social contracts, their alliances — around fossil fuel wealth? Do they adapt, or do they become the new failed states?

## Go deeper
- Compare the geopolitics of the *transitional phase* (fossil fuels still matter, but renewables are growing) versus a *post-transition world* — how should a country's strategy differ in each?
- Investigate the "critical materials" angle: which specific minerals are most concentrated in a few countries, and what new cartels or dependencies might emerge?
- Explore the coal blind spot: why do countries like Australia and China — heavily reliant on coal — get less attention in the literature, and what does that mean for their geopolitical trajectories?

10. **Sunk costs** — (noun, /sʌŋk kɒsts/) — resources already invested that cannot be recovered, which often distort decision-making. _Example: Fossil fuel exporters face sunk costs that make them resist the transition even as it becomes economically rational._

12. **Stranded assets** — (noun, /ˈstrændɪd ˈæsets/) — investments that lose value before their expected lifetime due to changing conditions. _Example: Oil fields and coal plants risk becoming stranded assets as renewable energy displaces fossil fuels._
