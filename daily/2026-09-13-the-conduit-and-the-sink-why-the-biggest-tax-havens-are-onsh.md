# The Conduit and the Sink: Why the Biggest Tax Havens Are Onshore

> Everyone pictures a palm-fringed island. The money mostly flows through Delaware, the Netherlands, and Ireland.

## Why this is interesting
The word "offshore" conjures secrecy and sunshine, yet the jurisdictions now moving the most corporate money are landlocked or thoroughly "onshore" — Delaware, South Dakota, Singapore, Luxembourg, Hong Kong [1]. A June 2018 study found that the dominant locations for corporate tax avoidance are major *onshore* international financial centres, not offshore ones, costing an estimated US$200 billion in lost annual tax revenues [1]. The geography of tax avoidance has quietly inverted, and the old mental map is now misleading.

## First principles
Start with the irreducible mechanic: a jurisdiction can make itself attractive to non-resident money by offering low or zero *effective* taxation, and it can do so either by charging nothing or by engineering rules that make income legally vanish [2]. An offshore financial centre is defined not by palm trees but by scale — a jurisdiction providing financial services to non-residents "on a scale that is incommensurate with the size and the financing of its domestic economy" [1]. "Offshore" therefore describes *whose* money it is (non-residents'), not *where* the place sits [1]. Two distinct species emerge: traditional havens with near-zero headline rates and few tax treaties (like Jersey), and modern corporate havens with non-zero headline rates, high OECD compliance, and large treaty networks whose BEPS tools push *de facto* rates toward zero [2].

## Break it into pieces
- **Conduit vs. Sink:** a 2017 study split OFCs into 24 "Sink" jurisdictions, where value disproportionately disappears, and five "Conduit" jurisdictions, through which value disproportionately *moves* toward the Sinks [1]. Why does the plumbing matter more than the drain?
- **Headline vs. effective rate:** the Netherlands (25%), the U.K. (19%), Singapore (17%), and Ireland (12.5%) all tax above zero, yet multinationals' effective rates net of BEPS tools approach zero [3]. What is the difference between a rate and a reality?
- **Respectability as infrastructure:** corporate havens market themselves as "knowledge economies" and IP as a "new economy" asset, which is encoded in statute as their primary BEPS tool [3]. Why does legitimacy buy access to treaties?
- **Substance requirements:** Singapore and Ireland require a "substantive presence," effectively an "employment tax" of roughly 2–3% of profits shielded [3]. Who really pays that?

## Follow the incentives
Each actor is chasing a different prize. Multinationals want to shift profits to where they are taxed least, using intricate schemes that survive even as individual evasion is squeezed [2]. Corporate havens want to be *chosen* — so they cultivate respectability, because a haven needs other jurisdictions to sign bilateral tax treaties that accept its BEPS tools [3]; traditional havens like Jersey, by contrast, charge zero and consequently have few treaties [2]. Onshore states bear the risk: they lose revenue, and estimates of the scale of taxes avoided vary [2]. Meanwhile, the IMF–OECD–FATF push from 2000 onward on common standards, regulatory compliance, and banking transparency has significantly weakened the *regulatory* attraction of OFCs [1]. The "substance" rules are a telling compromise: they impose a small real cost (jobs, roughly 2–3% of shielded profits) to buy legitimacy, and if those are real jobs, the tax is mitigated [3].

## How it echoes elsewhere
This is the same pattern as **arbitrage in any regulated system**: when rules differ across borders, value flows to the seam between them, and intermediaries who can stand in two jurisdictions at once capture the spread. It also echoes **network routing** — the Conduit/Sink distinction is essentially a hub-and-spoke topology, where traffic is valuable not at the destination but at the chokepoint that funnels it there [1]. In both cases, the strategic position is the *transit*, not the *terminus*.

## A real-world case
Ireland is the sharpest illustration. Zucman's "quantum of funds" ranking places Ireland as the largest global corporate tax haven, and in proxy tests it is the largest recipient of U.S. tax inversions (the U.K. is third, the Netherlands fifth) [3]. Its "double Irish" BEPS tool is credited with the largest build-up of untaxed corporate offshore cash in history [3]. Yet Ireland's headline corporate rate is 12.5% — above zero — and it requires substantive presence [3]. That combination is exactly the modern corporate-haven formula: a respectable rate, a treaty network, and a legal tool that makes income disappear before it is taxed.

## Second-order effects
If the biggest havens are onshore and treaty-rich, then transparency initiatives aimed at *secrecy* may miss the main game. The Common Reporting Standard and the end of Swiss-style banking secrecy substantially reduced individual tax evasion [2] — but "huge and complex corporations, like multinationals, can still shift profits to corporate tax havens using intricate schemes" [2]. My reading: the crackdown pushed avoidance up the complexity ladder, from individuals hiding accounts to corporations exploiting *legal* exemptions. A second consequence: economic-substance legislation, originally resisted on extraterritoriality, human-rights, privacy, and colonialism grounds, has ended up putting Cayman, BVI, and Bermuda "far ahead of onshore regulatory regimes" [3] — the regulated leapfrogged the regulators.

## A question to sit with
If a jurisdiction taxes above zero yet its users pay near zero, is it a tax haven — or simply a country whose rules are written to be used?

## Vocabulary Builder
1. **incommensurate** — (adjective, /ˌɪnkəˈmɛnʃərət/) — out of proportion; not matching in scale. _Example: An OFC hosts financial activity incommensurate with its tiny domestic economy._
2. **pejorative** — (adjective, /pɪˈdʒɒrətɪv/) — expressing contempt or disapproval. _Example: "Tax haven" is often used as a pejorative term._
3. **conduit** — (noun, /ˈkɒndjuɪt/) — a channel through which something passes. _Example: Conduit OFCs move value toward the Sink havens._
4. **base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS)** — (noun phrase, /beɪs ɪˈrəʊʒən/) — strategies that shrink a company's taxable base by moving profits to low-tax places. _Example: The double Irish is a classic BEPS tool._
5. **effective tax rate (ETR)** — (noun phrase, /ɪˈfɛktɪv tæks reɪt/) — the actual rate paid after deductions and schemes, as opposed to the headline rate. _Example: A 12.5% headline rate can yield an ETR near zero._
6. **tax-neutral** — (adjective, /tæks ˈnjuːtrəl/) — describing a structure where no tax is levied at the vehicle level, so tax is paid where investors reside. _Example: The Cayman exempted company is a tax-neutral vehicle._
7. **non-domiciled** — (adjective, /nɒn ˈdɒmɪsaɪld/) — not legally resident in a given jurisdiction for tax purposes. _Example: Havens offer low rates to non-domiciled investors._
8. **secrecy** — (noun, /ˈsiːkrəsi/) — the practice of keeping financial information hidden. _Example: The Common Reporting Standard was intended to end offshore financial secrecy._
9. **arbitrage** — (noun, /ˈɑːbɪtrɑːʒ/) — profiting from price or rule differences between two markets. _Example: Tax planning is a form of regulatory arbitrage._
10. **extraterritoriality** — (noun, /ˌɛkstrəˌtɛrɪtɔːriˈælɪti/) — the application of a jurisdiction's laws beyond its borders. _Example: Substance rules were resisted on extraterritoriality grounds._
11. **substantive presence** — (noun phrase, /səbˈstæntɪv ˈprɛzəns/) — a real operational footprint, such as staff and offices, in a jurisdiction. _Example: Ireland requires a substantive presence from corporates._
12. **inversion** — (noun, /ɪnˈvɜːʃən/) — a corporate move that relocates headquarters to a lower-tax country. _Example: Ireland is the largest recipient of U.S. tax inversions._
